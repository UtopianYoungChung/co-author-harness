# Redistributing Initiative: Agency, Delegation, and the Question of the Non-Human Actor

## Abstract

Initiative, oversight, and accountability are moving between human and non-human actors in organizational settings, and the vocabularies of *agency* and *delegation* have not kept pace. The gap is load-bearing: it shapes accountability, design, and the field's open questions.

In this paper, I inquire recent information systems (IS) and human–computer interaction (HCI) literature for how it conceptualizes agency and delegation, and I interrogate the hidden assumptions behind loaded terms: *autonomous*, *agentic*, *hybrid*, *emergence*, *delegation*. Agency is anchored across most of this literature in features traditionally associated with humans; I trace what happens as authors stretch those features onto non-human actors, or refuse the extension. I do not adopt the industry label *agentic AI* as the organizing object: its built-in assumptions are part of what this survey examines.

<mark style="background:#fff88f">That literature sorts into six descriptive framings: entity-focused, relational, constitutive, structural-emergence, practical-management, posthumanist design. Running through the phenomenon are three recurring tensions: the decomposition of agency from humanness versus the charge of a category mistake, the travel of loaded terms across incompatible framings, and unresolved continuity with earlier waves of automation.</mark>

On the first tension, I group the literature into three incompatible positions: dimensional decomposition, dissolution into relation or attribution, and reframing toward situated practice. Each bears on a concrete accountability gap the relational literature itself concedes. I close with a sharper articulation of the problem and open questions for later research, not with an endorsed framework or a verdict.

**Keywords:** agency; delegation; non-human actor; accountability; theoretical survey; agentic information systems; autonomous; emergence; agentic ai

## 1. Introduction

Something is being redistributed. In contemporary organizational settings, initiative and oversight and accountability are moving between human and non-human actors in ways that existing vocabularies strain to describe. Software built on large language models chains what developer documentation calls "tool calls" (Schick et al., 2023) without waiting for a prompt. Content-moderation pipelines process content at scales no human team could match, making decisions that platforms and EU regulators (Digital Services Act, 2022; AI Act, 2024) describe as consequential. "Human-in-the-loop" architectures leave genuinely unclear which party is in the loop and which is on it. The design-pattern naming itself reflects the contested vocabulary the paper sets out to interrogate. The industry label for systems of this kind is *agentic AI* or *agentic information systems*. This paper deliberately refuses to adopt that label as its organizing category.

"Agentic AI" is a recent, still-debated label whose built-in assumptions are part of what this survey needs to examine. Holldack et al. (2026) and Kuss and Meske (2025) give it prominent articulations. I read both as evidence of how the field is currently framing the problem. The underlying question, how to describe the redistribution of initiative between human and non-human actors, is older than the label and will likely outlast it.

The vocabulary currently in use (*agency*, *delegation*, *autonomy*, *agentic*) is unstable in ways that affect accountability, design, and theory. Three patterns run through that instability, and I trace each without trying to resolve it.

One is what I call the decomposition tension. Agency, in most of the literature, is anchored in features traditionally associated with humans: intentionality and the deliberative, reflective, and moral capacities that accompany it. The literature fractures over whether those features can be separated from humanness at all. Some positions attempt the decomposition. Others dissolve the question by relocating agency out of entities. Others refuse it entirely and redirect inquiry toward situated practice. These are incompatible commitments rather than different emphases within a shared view. Whether the decomposition is substantive or stipulative remains open, and I do not adjudicate it here.

A second pattern is what I call the travel of loaded terms. *Autonomous*, *agentic*, *hybrid*, *delegation*, *emergence* shift meaning between authors, each one carrying commitments the next author may not share. Characterizing what each term smuggles in, and flagging where terms fail to travel, is part of setting the problem rather than a question I answer.

A third pattern: the literature lacks, on its own resources, the historical apparatus needed to separate genuine discontinuity from the recurrence of older automation problems in fresh vocabulary. That absence is itself worth flagging for later work.

**Structure.** Section 2 unpacks five terms I cannot adopt without first marking what they import. Section 3 lays out six descriptive framings in the literature and traces the commitments each author's vocabulary drags along; that is the second pattern, the travel of loaded terms. Section 4, the longest section, takes up the decomposition tension by grouping three incompatible positions the extended literature already articulates: Decomposition, following Dattathrani and De' (2023); Dissolution, following Leonardi (2023, 2025) and Kuss and Meske (2025); and Reframing, following Suchman (2006), Baumer et al. (2024), and Ratto et al. (2024). Section 5 anchors the discussion in a concrete accountability gap conceded by Holldack himself, reads each of the three positions against that gap, and refines the problem statement in light of the preceding analysis. Section 6 closes with open questions for later research.

## 2. Terms to Hold at Arm's Length

There are five terms I cannot adopt without first marking what they import: *agency*, *autonomous*, *hybrid*, *emergence*, and *delegation*. Each travels between authors carrying different commitments, and each use in what follows is tagged with the sense in play.

**Agency.** The word is used across the literature in at least four different senses. Bandura (2001) supplies a psychological definition of agency as the capability to influence one's functioning and the course of events, where *capability* is the individual agentive capacity to act, distinct from the organizational-structural competence that Calhau et al.'s (2023, 2024) KSA vocabulary will denote in Sec. 3. Baird and Maruping (2021) offer an IS-specific behavioral definition: "the ability to accept rights and responsibilities for ambiguous tasks and outcomes under uncertainty and to decide and act autonomously" (p. 316).

Leonardi (2023) relocates the concept entirely, explicitly revising his 2011 imbrication framework (the interlocking of human and material agencies into situated practice). He writes that "agency is not a property of anything, nor does it belong to anyone. Agency materializes as a relation in its own right" (p. xvi). Baumer et al. (2024) take a constitutive view in which the subject of agency is produced by its interactions rather than prior to them. These are not four phrasings of the same idea. They pick out different phenomena.

Nor are these senses symmetric in their cognitivist commitments. The Bandura, Baird–Maruping, and Leonardi senses each anchor agency in features developed within deliberative-cognitivist models of mind, even where Leonardi (2023) redistributes them across human–material relations rather than placing them in a single actor. Baumer et al.'s constitutive sense relocates the question without rejecting the cognitivist anchor outright. Suchman's situated-action sense, treated in Section 4.3, refuses the anchor by construction. The asymmetry will be consequential when Section 4 contrasts Position C with Positions A and B.

**Autonomous.** Possible readings include complete freedom of action, absence of human intervention, capacity for unprompted initiation, and unsupervised execution of pre-specified goals. Each picks out a different phenomenon and implies a different accountability story. When Holldack et al. (2026) describe systems as "autonomous," they usually mean unsupervised execution of pre-specified goals. When Leonardi (2025) writes of "autonomous AI," he means something closer to unprompted initiation. The word travels between these senses without notice in most of the literature.

**Hybrid / human-in/on-the-loop.** What is being hybridized? Decision-making, cognitive labor, responsibility, perception? What is the "loop"? Ratto et al. (2024) press the question hardest.

**Emergence.** Calhau et al. (2023, 2024) use the word in a formal system-science sense, grounded in Bunge and General Systems Theory, to refer to system-level properties that arise from the interaction of components. Relational theorists use the word differently, to describe how agency comes into being in the encounter between actors. The two usages should not be allowed to travel without notice.

**Delegation.** Presupposes a delegator with standing to transfer rights and responsibilities. Holldack et al.'s (2026) three delegation patterns (user-invoked, bidirectional, IS-invoked) require stable agents at both ends of the transfer. Under Baumer et al.'s (2024) constitutive view, and under Suchman's (2006) empirical one, the boundary between delegator and delegate is produced by the very interaction in which delegation is supposed to occur. The word prejudges what is at stake.

These five sense-audits do not stand alone. Section 3 shows that they combine into four vocabulary problems persisting across all six major framings of the literature, and Section 4 traces how three incompatible positions inherit those problems rather than resolving them.

**A note on the register the survey does not enter.** Each of the five terms above has a stipulated, operationalizable definition in the formal multi-agent-systems literature: *delegation* as task allocation under a coordination protocol, *autonomy* as decision authority over a defined choice space, *emergence* as system-level behavior arising from local agent rules, and so on. The vocabulary surveyed in this paper is contested in part because it refuses the operational reductions that stabilize the same words in the formal-agent tradition. The instability Section 2 audits is, in this register-boundary light, the cost the IS/HCI literature pays for retaining phenomena (intentionality, endorsement, situated practice) the formal tradition stipulates away. I do not enter that tradition's register here; I mark it because the paper's diagnosis of unstable vocabulary is sharper if the alternative register is named rather than unmentioned.

## 3. Framing the Literature and Its Loaded Vocabulary

The six framings below emerged from a reading organized around where each source locates agency: in entities, in relations, in interactions that constitute their relata, in system-level emergence, in the management of human–system relationships, or in posthumanist critique of "the human." One evaluative thread runs through all six: the accountability gap conceded within the relational literature itself, which recurs as the lens Sec. 4 applies to each position and as the anchoring question of Sec. 5.

**Entity-focused framings (Holldack et al., 2026; Kuss and Meske, 2025).** Entity-focused framings subdivide into *inherent* and *behavioral* views. The inherent view requires mental states (consciousness, intentionality, free will) as conditions on agency. On this reading, IS artifacts do not possess agency in their own right, and their apparent agency is understood as a proxy enacting the intentions of human principals. The view assumes, without arguing for it, that agency is anchored in human-specific mental properties.

The behavioral view (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Ågerfalk, 2020, as cited in Holldack et al., 2026, and others) defines agency by action-capacity under uncertainty without requiring mental properties. The hidden assumption is that "action" can be identified without presupposing intentionality, a commitment some philosophers of action would contest.

**Relational framings.** Leonardi's revised position (2023) moves away from his 2011 imbrication framework toward the view that agency materializes *as* a relation rather than residing in either party prior to the encounter. Strong relational and sociomaterial framings treat social and material elements as inseparable and co-constituted in practice. They locate agency in the sociomaterial configuration without distinguishing entity-specific contributions. The most systematic development runs through Barad's (2007) agential realism, as summarized by Kuss and Meske (2025): an ontology in which agency is not a property of entities but a property of the intra-actions through which entities are produced. The hidden assumption, which Holldack et al. (2026) themselves flag, is that this framing "does not provide a framework for differentiating how agency is distributed across individual entities, thereby blurring responsibility boundaries and hindering accountable assignments of control" (p. 7). I will call that flagged limitation the accountability gap: the relational view's conceded inability to differentiate how agency distributes across individual entities. It anchors Sec. 5.

Leonardi (2025) pivots again, treating agency as *attribution* and describing "agency loops" through which power circulates.

**Constitutive framings.** Baumer et al. (2024) argue that "human" and "algorithm" are categories that "only come into being through their mutual interconnections and interactions" (p. 2). The argument is explicitly grounded in Barad's (2007) intra-action and agential cut: entities reside in indeterminate potentiality until a cut is enacted through interaction, and the cut constitutes both what observes and what is observed. Underneath sits a post-structuralist ontology in which subjects are constituted by relations rather than prior to them. I mark that commitment as a position, not a conclusion.

**Structural-emergence framings.** Calhau et al. (2023, 2024) work at a different scale from the others, modeling how capabilities and competences emerge from organizational assemblages within enterprise architecture. The 2024 paper grounds this explicitly in human KSAs: knowledge, skills, and attitudes. *Capability* in Calhau's sense is the organizational-structural competence emergent from the KSA assemblage, and is distinct from the individual capability-to-influence sense Bandura (2001) uses to define *agency* in Section 2. The hidden assumption becomes important in Section 4: the KSA vocabulary is not neutral. It imports a human-resource-management genealogy, and "attitude" in particular presupposes a psychological-state-bearing actor. Whether the framework can extend to non-human participants without equivocation remains a live question.

**Practical-management framings.** Wissuchek et al. (2025) provide an empirical catalogue of challenges in managing the human–agent relationship. Their catalogue surfaces four challenge categories: *metaknowledge* (humans and systems systematically misjudge each other's capabilities), *delegation* (user-invoked, bidirectional, and IS-invoked patterns disrupt traditional task ownership; the three-way carving parallels older multi-agent-systems taxonomies of master-slave, peer-to-peer, and autonomous-initiation delegation), *human-centricity* (workers fear loss of professional superiority as the locus of initiative shifts), and *accountability* (distributed agency across human–AI constellations leaves no single party bearing full responsibility).

Each category is documented empirically. Fügener et al. (2021) find delegation accuracy deteriorates when task complexity exceeds users' capacity to evaluate AI reasoning. Stelmaszak et al. (2024), discussed in detail below, supply the canonical case in which formal accountability survives the loss of task-initiation rights. Seeber et al. (2020) frame the broader challenge as "machines as teammates."

Holldack et al. (2026) organize similar observations into a multi-level taxonomy (individual, organizational, societal), arguing that organizations lack the defined roles, control mechanisms, and governance frameworks required to integrate self-directed AI "team members."

The practical-management framings supply the empirical terrain that the preceding theoretical framings, entity-focused, relational, constitutive, and structural-emergence, are each trying to explain. What Holldack et al. (2026, p. 8) call a "metaknowledge challenge" is what Suchman (2006, ch. 8–9) describes empirically as the failure of humans and machines to stabilize mutual affordances. What Wissuchek et al. (2025) call a "human-centricity challenge" is what Ratto et al. (2024) would cast as an unexamined model of "the human."

The assumption shared across these framings is that the object of management is a stable "relationship" between sufficiently distinct parties, a move the constitutive framings and Suchman contest.

**Posthumanist design framings.** Ratto et al. (2024) interrogate which features of "the human" are being modeled when generative AI is designed to emulate people. I use *posthumanism* here in the sense of the critical denaturalization of "the human" as a category prior to design, not in the sense of the literary or science-fiction uses of the term. Their central empirical finding: across 326,979 ACM papers mentioning LLMs and "human-like," "human-like" is almost never defined. The project assumes, without making it explicit, that "the human" can be denaturalized without presupposing something stable on the other side of the denaturalization.

The six framings disagree on three points: where agency lives (in entities, in relations, in assemblages, or in ascriptions); whether "human" and "non-human" are prior to the analysis or produced by it; and at what scale the question should be posed. The carvings do not line up. Holldack et al. (2026) present entity-focused and relational as the primary organizing split. Kuss and Meske (2025) refine it into a three-way scheme (inherent, behavioral, relational). Neither fits Baumer et al.'s (2024) constitutive view cleanly, and neither accommodates the speed at which Leonardi has moved within it: 2011 imbrication, 2023 "agency materializes," 2025 agency-as-*attribution*. I read three positions in fifteen years from a single author as a data point about the instability of the relational framing.

### What the framings leave unresolved

The framings disagree on substance. They also reproduce, at the level of the carving, four vocabulary problems that the Section 2 sense-audits surfaced one term at a time. Each problem cross-cuts the framings rather than belonging to any one of them, and each is a conceptual turn-point the Section 4 positions will rest on.

**Problem 1: The entity/relational carving itself.** The most common organizing scheme in the IS literature splits agency into entity-focused and relational. The Section 2 audit on *Agency* shows why the binary cannot carry: the four senses *Agency* picks out (psychological, behavioral, relational, constitutive) do not collapse onto two slots. Baumer et al.'s (2024) constitutive view fits neither side. Structural-emergence and posthumanist design fit neither side neatly either. The carving organizes the literature without describing it.

**Problem 2: Delegation under unstable parties.** *Delegation* (audited in Section 2) presupposes stable agents at both ends of the transfer. The Uber case (Stelmaszak et al., 2024) is the site where that assumption visibly breaks. Algorithmic delegation stripped drivers of task-initiation rights while retaining their formal accountability, and the boundary between delegator and executor shifted with each system update. The word imports a stability the system does not supply. The formal multi-agent-systems vocabulary tracks the same phenomenon directly under the heading of *incentive-incompatibility*: a register the IS/HCI literature intersects but does not adopt, leaving the destabilization unnamed at the level the design failure occupies.

**Problem 3: "Human-like" as a floating design category.** "Human-like" functions as a design target whose referent is rarely fixed. Ratto et al. (2024) find it almost never defined across the 326,979 ACM papers that mention LLMs and "human-like." The Section 2 audit on *Hybrid* names a parallel slippage (what is being combined goes unspecified), and Ratto presses that question hardest. The category does work in design discourse precisely because its referent floats. Where Problem 1 concerns how the field organizes its theoretical positions, Problem 3 concerns how design practice construes its target. The two operate at different levels and do not collapse.

**Problem 4: Attribution as a fifth sense of agency.** Leonardi (2025) shifts from ontology to performativity: agency is what attributions of agency *do*. The move inherits commitments from social-psychology research on sense of agency (Haggard & Tsakiris, 2009) that do not sit cleanly with the sociomaterial vocabulary Leonardi retains. Read against the Section 2 *Agency* audit, this is a fifth sense added to the four already in play, relocating the instability rather than resolving it.

Each framing is internally coherent but carries commitments other authors in the literature reject. The instability of the vocabulary is itself part of the problem I set. Those six framings, and the four vocabulary problems they trail, stage the three incompatible positions that Section 4 now characterizes.

## 4. The Decomposition Tension in the Literature

The three incompatible positions the literature already takes on whether agency can be decomposed from humanness each carry a distinct posture toward the accountability gap Holldack concedes, which Sec. 5 anchors in the wider problem. The following table maps the three positions' commitments.


| Position                                                              | Agency Source                                                   | Accountable Locus                                                       | Primary Methodology                                                   |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A. Decomposition (Dattathrani & De', 2023)                            | Separable dimensions of traditionally human properties          | The dimension the analyst credits (in practice: *endorsement*)          | Philosophical decomposition into six dimensions; field illustration   |
| B.Dissolution (Leonardi, 2023, 2025; Kuss & Meske, 2025)              | Sociomaterial relation or circulating attribution, not entities | Distributed across agency loops; accountability conceded as unresolved  | Relational ontology (agential realism); attribution-politics analysis |
| C. Reframing (Suchman, 2006; Baumer et al., 2024; Ratto et al., 2024) | Produced in situated practice through intra-action              | Emergent in the practice that mobilizes the human/non-human distinction | Empirical investigation of how categories become salient              |


Suchman (2006) supplies a diagnostic worth surfacing before the exposition: "it is actually the persistence of the human-machine divide rather than its disappearance that makes the prospect of machine autonomy so compelling" (p. 213). The line illuminates why the literature oscillates between Positions A and B rather than settling: each side's argument depends on the very divide the other side claims to have dissolved. Three positions rather than two becomes legible against this diagnostic; Position C names the move that breaks the oscillation by refusing the divide as antecedent rather than dramatizing or dissolving it.

Two questions organize the exposition that follows, and the literature has not settled either. The first is whether the features traditionally associated with humans (intentionality, consciousness, deliberation, moral standing, capacity for goal-formation) can be decomposed and coherently attributed to non-human actors. The second is whether "non-human agency" is itself a category mistake whose apparent plausibility depends on the quiet retention of human features. For each position I trace the commitments it imports and the methodological moves it authorizes.

**4.1 Position A: Decomposition (Dattathrani & De', 2023).** Dattathrani and De' (2023) perform the decomposition more explicitly than anyone else in the literature. Drawing on philosophy of action, they identify six dimensions of agency, with the sixth, *consciousness*, subdivided into its *access* and *phenomenal* aspects.


| Dimension                                                                                                                    | Philosophical Grounding                                                                                                           |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| *Passivity*                                                                                                                  | —                                                                                                                                 |
| *Automaticity*                                                                                                               | —                                                                                                                                 |
| *Rationality*                                                                                                                | Davidson on rationalization (as cited in Dattathrani & De', 2023)                                                                 |
| *Endorsement*                                                                                                                | Bratman and Velleman on endorsement and self-governance; Frankfurt on higher-order volition (as cited in Dattathrani & De', 2023) |
| *Freedom-to-choose*                                                                                                          | O'Connor and Clarke on freedom (as cited in Dattathrani & De', 2023)                                                              |
| *Consciousness*: *access* (the functional, reportable integration of information) and *phenomenal* (first-person experience) | Chalmers (1995) on consciousness                                                                                                  |


They illustrate with a field case that runs from a thermal sensor through a thermal imaging system to an AI cancer-detection pipeline and finally to a human technician. From this they argue that agency can be attributed to non-human systems *in degree*. A system may exhibit access consciousness, rudimentary rationality, and some automaticity while lacking phenomenal consciousness and meaningful endorsement.

This is a genuine attempt to extract properties separable from humanness. Three questions are worth pressing against it.

One: is the decomposition substantive or stipulative? Dattathrani and De' plausibly carve out separable capacities in some dimensions (passivity, automaticity). In others, especially endorsement, they do substantial accountability work while quietly importing the reflective self-governance Velleman (1992) and Bratman (2000) build into the concept (as cited in Dattathrani & De', 2023). The point cuts twice. Bratman's concept has also been operationalized in the formal-agent tradition (Belief–Desire–Intention architectures), where intentions are reduced to functional roles and the reflective load is dropped — a parallel debate in which the same source-author yields incompatible derivations, and which Position A's dimension scheme could productively engage rather than treat the philosophical reading as the only one in play.

Two: does treating phenomenal consciousness as a *dimension* alongside the others risk smuggling the hard problem past as if it were a tunable parameter? Chalmers's (1995) own view is precisely that phenomenal consciousness is *not* continuous with the functional dimensions Dattathrani and De' place next to it.

Three: do Dattathrani and De' close Holldack's accountability gap, or only relocate it? On one reading, they relocate it. The question of who is accountable for a distributed system's action becomes the question of who exhibits sufficient *endorsement* over which portion of its behavior, where endorsement carries the reflective-ratification load specified above, not the analyst's after-the-fact *attribution*. On this reading, the question becomes which human principal Dattathrani and De's scheme locates as the site of reflective self-governance over the relevant decision.

Fügener et al. (2021) document precisely a setting where endorsement becomes practically consequential. In their study of human-in-the-loop designs, they find that human operators can degrade into passive ratifiers of algorithmic output, which they term the "borg" risk. The finding suggests that the endorsement dimension, far from being a stable attribute, is eroded by the very delegation patterns it is supposed to anchor. The gap does not disappear. It migrates into a single dimension where it becomes harder to notice.

Dattathrani and De' themselves critique ANT (Latour, 2005), Barad (2007), and Leonardi (2011) for flattening or dissolving distinctions their scheme preserves. That critique maps directly onto Position B's ontological move and stages the two positions as genuinely incompatible commitments, not different emphases.

**4.2 Position B: Dissolution (Leonardi, 2023, 2025; Kuss & Meske, 2025).** The dissolution position relocates agency from entities to relations or to attributions, and on that basis claims the decomposition tension dissolves: agency was never in the actor, so there is nothing to decompose. Leonardi's 2023 revision of his own imbrication framework is the cleanest statement: "agency is not a property of anything, nor does it belong to anyone. Agency materializes as a relation in its own right" (p. xvi). Kuss and Meske (2025) formalize this via Barad's agential realism into a systematic alternative to entity-centric ontologies, arguing that the relational perspective is underrepresented in IS research because of its ontological complexity rather than because of any flaw in its reasoning.

Leonardi (2025) pivots once more. Agency is a matter of *attribution*, circulating through "agency loops": recursive patterns of delegation, attribution, contingency, reassertion, and reconfiguration. This move occupies a different ontological register from the Barad-grounded agential realism that Kuss and Meske formalize. Leonardi (2025) is performativist and social-psychological: agency is produced through the act of attribution, not prior to it. Kuss and Meske (2025) is ontological. I group both under "Dissolution" because both relocate agency away from entities, but the internal heterogeneity should not be read as unity. The paradox Leonardi fastens on is striking: "the more autonomous AI appears, the less autonomous humans feel" (Leonardi, 2025, p. 1).

Kellogg et al. (2019) supply empirical terrain for this paradox. Their study of algorithms at work documents how algorithmic control contests established managerial authority, redistributing the capacity to direct, evaluate, and discipline workers in ways that no single party fully owns. Position B would describe this redistribution as agency circulating through attribution loops.

Two questions press against this position. One: does the relocation pay its bills? Holldack himself concedes it does not: the strong-relational view "does not provide a framework for differentiating how agency is distributed across individual entities" (p. 7). Kuss and Meske acknowledge the same limitation while treating it as a feature. If the accountability question is real, the literature has not settled whether the inability to answer it counts as a methodological cost or a methodological feature. Two: the relational framing does not generate stable commitments so much as continually regenerate the problem it was supposed to dissolve. Leonardi (2025) is frank: dissolving entity-level agency does not close the accountability question. It *politicizes* it.

**4.3 Position C: Reframing / Refusal (Suchman, 2006; Baumer et al., 2024; Ratto et al., 2024).** Suchman supplies the cleanest statement of the third position: "discussions of nonhuman agency need to be reframed from categorical debates to empirical investigations of the concrete practices through which categories of human and nonhuman are mobilized and become salient within particular fields of action" (p. 1). The question shifts "from one of whether humans and machines are the same or different to how and when the categories of human or machine become relevant" (p. 2).

Position C's methodological move is visible when the Stelmaszak Uber case (Section 3) is re-read under a Position C lens. The categories *driver* and *dispatch algorithm* are not stable parties on either side of a pre-existing transfer. Each system update reconfigures what counts as initiative, what counts as ratification, and where the boundary between the two is drawn. The methodological question Position C presses is not which party holds the agency but how, in the textures of that specific practice, the distinction between them comes to matter.

Two parts of Suchman's apparatus do the work this re-reading depends on. The first is the plan/situated-action distinction (Suchman, 2006): plans are resources for action, not determinants of it, and action's intelligibility is achieved in situ through ongoing reciprocal sense-making with the environment. The dispatching algorithm in the Stelmaszak case functions as a plan in Suchman's sense; the driver's improvisation is the situated action; the design failure consists in the system's refusal to treat the plan as a resource and its insistence on treating it as a determinant. Position C is not "practice all the way down" as a slogan but the specific methodological commitment that situated action's structure cannot be derived from the plan that anticipates it.

Baumer et al. converge from a constitutive route: "human" and "algorithm" are produced through their interactions. Their (2024) constitutive ontology draws on Barad's (2007) agential realism. In Barad's framework, agency is not a property an entity possesses but a doing, specifically an enactment emerging through intra-action. Intra-action differs from interaction: the relata come into being through the engagement rather than existing prior to it.

The second part of Suchman's apparatus distinguishes her from this Baradian register. Suchman's *configuration* (the term that retitles the 2006 edition) names an arrangement that produces the human and the machine as such within a specific practice, but it retains the relata as practical achievements rather than collapsing them into intra-action. Position C's situated-practice methodology accordingly does not require Position B's ontological commitment, and the slippage between them — Baumer et al. straddle, Leonardi 2025 oscillates — is what the configuration/intra-action distinction stabilizes.

Ratto et al. (2024) converge from a posthumanist design route: the category "human-like" is almost never defined in the design literature that relies on it.

Position C is neither decomposition nor dissolution. It is a methodological redirection. Refuse to answer the decomposition tension in the abstract, and instead examine how the distinction is drawn in situated practice. The persistence diagnostic introduced at Section 4 opening does its most pointed work in this specific setting: "autonomy" is not the dissolution of the human-machine boundary but its insistence, in a form designed to make non-human action dramatic.

Two questions should be pressed against this position. One: is the refusal to answer from first principles a helpful discipline or a way of declining to engage the decomposition tension at all? Suchman would reply that the tension is malformed, and that the productive move is to trace the practices in which "human" and "machine" are mobilized, not to legislate categories in advance. This is a reasonable reply, but it leaves the tension intact for any research program not committed to ethnomethodological redescription. Two: does empirical description proceed without categorical commitments? It does not, fully. To describe a practice is already to individuate actors, events, and boundaries. Suchman is more aware of this than most. The literature has not settled whether that awareness dissolves the tension or merely marks it.

## 5. What Follows: Accountability and the Refined Problem

Holldack concedes directly that the strong-relational view "does not provide a framework for differentiating how agency is distributed across individual entities, thereby blurring responsibility boundaries and hindering accountable assignments of control" (p. 7). This is a concrete, citable limitation, conceded by the author who does the most to take relational framings seriously. Kuss and Meske (2025) acknowledge the same limitation. What the surveyed literature does not supply, and does not acknowledge as absent, is any apparatus for decomposing delegation into its constituent role-obligations.

Calhau et al. (2023, 2024) offer formal machinery for distributing causal contribution across system parts, but their KSA primitives presuppose human actors, which leaves the gap unclosed without substantive reworking. Behavioral entity-focused authors (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Ågerfalk, 2020) offer clean attribution at the cost of defining agency as whatever-can-be-attributed. The three positions in Section 4 each load accountability somewhere the framework cannot fully inspect, into endorsement, into the politics of attribution, or into situated practice, and none supplies the apparatus Holldack's concession asks for.

**The practical implication, stated plainly.** When one of these systems makes a consequential decision and something goes wrong, the field cannot yet say cleanly who answers for it. Position A points to whoever holds reflective endorsement over the act; Fügener et al. (2021) show endorsement erodes precisely under the delegation patterns that make the question pressing. Position B distributes the answer across an "agency loop," leaving no single party fully on the hook, which is the gap Holldack concedes. Position C declines a general answer and asks how, in this specific practice, accountability is being assigned. None of these gives a regulator, a designer, or a complainant the kind of footing that older legal and organizational vocabularies (principal-and-agent, employer-of-record, the captain of the ship) took for granted. The closer a system operates to self-direction, the further the available vocabulary drifts from the one accountability institutions are built to handle.


| Position           | Where accountability lands                            | Practical hazard                                                                                          |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A. Decomposition   | The actor holding reflective endorsement over the act | "borg" risk: endorsement erodes under delegation, leaving formal accountability stranded (Fügener et al., 2021) |
| B. Dissolution     | Distributed across an agency loop; no single locus    | Conceded gap: no apparatus for differentiating contribution across entities (Holldack et al., 2026)       |
| C. Reframing       | Empirically traced in the practice as it unfolds      | No general rule; ill-suited to ex ante regulation or stable organizational role assignment                |


No single framework in the literature supplies a general answer to how accountability should be distributed under conditions of redistributed initiative. Different frameworks suit different accountability questions, and I borrow selectively. I reach for Position A when the question is whether a specific system exhibits enough of a specific agency-dimension to warrant attribution, Position B when the question is how power and attribution circulate across a sociotechnical assemblage, and Position C when the question is how the categories themselves are being deployed in a specific practice. None of them answers all the questions, and I am not embarrassed to say so.

The vocabulary used to describe the redistribution of initiative between human and non-human actors is not merely *contested*. It is **unstable in three patterned ways**.

One pattern: the concept of agency is pulled in three incompatible directions at once (decomposition, dissolution, refusal), and the choice between them is doing accountability work none of the framings fully owns.

A second pattern: loaded terms (*autonomous*, *agentic*, *hybrid*, *delegation*, *emergence*) travel between authors carrying incompatible commitments, and the entity-focused/relational split commonly used to organize the field obscures positions that fit neither side.

A third pattern: the field's historical apparatus is too thin to separate genuine novelty from the recurrence of older automation problems in fresh vocabulary.

The third pattern warrants at least one concrete illustration before it is handed to Q-γ. Bainbridge's (1983) "ironies of automation" document the vocabulary instability one generation earlier. As industrial automation removed operators from direct control, the discussion shifted from *how to automate* to *how to keep humans in the loop*, preserving the vocabulary of agency and delegation while evacuating its referent. Accountability questions about supervision, degraded skill, and absent expertise settled into the same structurally unresolved state the current literature reaches with agentic systems. The expert-systems literature of the 1980s–90s supplies a second specimen. Systems marketed as capturing and externalizing human expertise generated immediate debate about whether the *knowledge* so captured was *the expert's*, *the system's*, or an artifact of the elicitation process: the same question the decomposition tension poses in Section 4, in a different vocabulary. Whether these recurrences are the same problem under a new label or genuinely distinct phenomena that superficially resemble one another is precisely what the field's current literature cannot say, because the historical apparatus required to make that determination is not assembled here. That inability is itself a finding the third pattern names.

These are three patterns in one underlying instability: a vocabulary stretched past the humans it was developed to describe.

The Section 4 mapping makes visible three tensions worth registering. First, two classes of properties come apart in the literature without a principled criterion for the cut. Some properties seem separable from humanness: environmental responsiveness, action-capacity under uncertainty, access consciousness in Dattathrani and De's sense. Others resist separation: phenomenal consciousness, endorsement, moral standing. Second, each of the three positions I examined loads accountability into a place that is hard to inspect (Position A into *endorsement*, Position B into attribution politics, Position C into situated practice). Third, Haslam's folk-concept data suggests the philosophical decompositions and the ordinary-language concept of agency may already have come apart.

One register sits just outside the survey, and naming it sharpens the four-way mapping the analysis has produced. Position A pays for decomposition with the question of whether the carving is substantive or stipulative; Position B pays for dissolution with the conceded accountability gap; Position C pays for refusal with the methodological cost of declining general answers; the formal multi-agent-systems register, sitting outside the surveyed literature, pays for operational stability by stipulating away the very phenomena (intentionality, endorsement, situated practice) the surveyed positions exist to address. Read together, the IS/HCI vocabulary's instability is the cost the field pays for refusing the operational reductions that would stabilize it. The instability is real. Whether it counts as a defect or a load-bearing feature depends on which register one is trying to occupy.

## 6. Open Questions Pointing Forward

Four open questions follow. They are candidate directions later research could take up, not problems this paper resolves.

- **Q-α (decomposition criterion).** What principled criterion, if any, could the literature supply for distinguishing the properties of agency that are separable from humanness from those that are not? Dattathrani and De' (Position A, 2023) supply dimensions but not a criterion; authors working in Positions B and C deny one is needed. A research inquiry focused on problem definition would need to commit to a candidate criterion and trace its tradeoffs against alternatives. This question is the most direct continuation of Section 4 and is the candidate most ready to be sharpened into a tractable technical research problem.
- **Q-β (accountability under redistributed initiative).** Where does accountability actually go when entity-level agency is dissolved or relocated? Holldack and Kuss & Meske both concede the relational view does not say. Leonardi (2025) describes the loops it leaves open but does not close them. A later inquiry could ask what an accountability framework that takes the relational view seriously *without* abandoning entity-level attribution would have to look like. This is a design problem as much as a theoretical one, and one that Agent-Oriented programs (e.g., AORE) organizing delegation around explicit role-obligations may eventually illuminate.
- **Q-γ (continuity apparatus).** What historical apparatus would the field need in order to tell genuine discontinuity from recurrence of older automation problems in new vocabulary? Closing it is a separate research task that would require sustained engagement with sources (Bainbridge, 1983; Suchman, 2006; the expert-systems literature; the history of bureaucratic delegation) the current literature only gestures at.
- **Q-δ (the live option the literature refuses).** None of the three positions in Section 4 takes seriously the view that "non-human agency" is a category mistake. A focused research inquiry could ask, neutrally, what the costs and benefits would be of restricting "agency" to human actors and developing alternative vocabulary for what systems do. This is the open question the literature most consistently declines to pose. Q-δ is itself posed against Position C: an inquiry pursuing it commits to a register in which "human" and "non-human" remain available as pre-analytic categories, which is the very commitment Suchman's reframing refuses. Pursuing Q-δ is therefore a Position-A or Position-B inquiry; framing it as neutral would be a category mistake of its own.

The four questions are not of equal shape. Q-β is design-adjacent and would pair naturally with empirical case work. Q-γ demands historical scholarship outside the current literature and is the least self-contained. Q-δ is the question the field most consistently declines to pose, and for that reason, may be the most generative.

Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics (RQM; 1996, 2017) is absent from the IS/HCI literature on agency. The core commitment of RQM is a radical generalization of the Einsteinian program: where special relativity demonstrates that simultaneity is observer-relative, RQM holds that all variable properties of a system are observer-relative, existing only in relation to other systems (Rovelli, 1996). There is no observer-independent description of a system's state; no vantage from which the question "what happened?" carries an absolute answer. The entities of the physical world do not precede their interactions; relations ground the notion of a thing rather than the reverse. The world described by RQM is a world of events, of localizable interactions, not a world of objects bearing intrinsic properties between interactions. The interpretation has been developed philosophically by van Fraassen, Bitbol, and Dorato, among others (Rovelli, 2017).

The relevance to Q-β is structural. The accountability gap Holldack concedes (the relational view provides no framework for differentiating how agency distributes across individual entities) is precisely the gap an observer-relative ontology would render constitutive rather than contingent. Asking "who decided?" presupposes observer-independent individuation of the decision-event, which RQM denies. Barad's agential realism, already in this paper's surveyed literature via Kuss and Meske (2025) and Baumer et al. (2024), is a Bohr-grounded predecessor: intra-action collapses the observer/observed cut. RQM operates on different ground. It retains distinct systems while making their properties mutually relative, a structure that maps more directly onto the delegator/delegatee asymmetry. Which observer-system the "decision" is well-defined relative to is not a question with a privileged answer. I offer this as a candidate direction for Q-β work, not as a commitment this paper undertakes.

**A closing note on the label.** *Agentic AI* may or may not survive the next revision cycle. More durable is the problem I have tried to set. The vocabulary of agency was developed to describe humans, and it is now being stretched, dissolved, and refused in incompatible ways as it is brought to bear on systems that mix human and non-human contribution. The field's accountability questions turn on an instability the field has not yet acknowledged is one. Something is being redistributed. The question for the next stage of work is whether the vocabulary of agency can name what that something is, or whether a different vocabulary serves the field better: one closer to practice and less freighted with humanist assumptions. Suchman's (2006) epilogue is the most developed sketch of such a vocabulary the field already has on the shelf; whether to take it up, refine it, or develop an alternative remains open.
