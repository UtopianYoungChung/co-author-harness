# Indirect Control and AI-Mediated Work: Management's Structural Contradiction at the Darlington Nuclear Generating Station

**Young Jo(seph) Chung**
Faculty of Information, University of Toronto | [jo.chung@utoronto.ca](mailto:jo.chung@utoronto.ca)
INF3130 — HCI | Milestone 5 (v3, trimmed) | April 27, 2026

## Abstract

Why do AI-driven workplace systems often feel helpful and heavy-handed at once? Matt Vidal names this tension the *management contradiction*: management's structural pull to coordinate workers for efficiency while disciplining them for *valorisation*. Under *indirect control*, the contradiction is pushed onto workers themselves.

For Menz and Nies, the crux is *legitimation*: workload is no longer justified by Taylorist appeals to merit and scientific calculation but by appeals to market pressure treated as self-evident necessity.

At Ontario Power Generation's Darlington Nuclear Generating Station, I worked with a Health and Safety Information System from 2017 to 2021 as a non-union management intermediary. Workers and intermediaries absorbed the gap between system counts and floor practice through *articulation work* that the dashboards never tracked. Philip Agre's *grammars of action* name the operational mechanism.

I call this displacement *bounded intensification*: automation absorbs managerial shortcuts, pushing reconciliation onto whoever stands at the boundary between rules and work. When *satisficing* migrates from a manager's ongoing judgment into rule-based routines, that boundary becomes someone's daily burden. The contribution is empirical: these dynamics are already active at the automation baseline, not waiting at some future *agentic* frontier. The management contradiction is not an organizational problem solvable by better design. It is a fundamental labour cost the architecture conceals.

## 1. Introduction

On a Tuesday morning in 2019, an incident report reached me through the Health and Safety Information System I worked with at Ontario Power Generation's Darlington Nuclear Generating Station. By the time I opened it, the system had already flagged it. It had categorized it, routed it to the right compliance officer, and assigned a preliminary risk score. It was doing exactly what it was built to do: coordinating safety information across a workforce of roughly 4,000 on one of the largest refurbishment projects in Canadian industrial history.

Yet the incident refused the system's categories. The worker who filed it described a near-miss and an improvised workaround. That was situated judgment. It kept a crew safe but broke a standardized rule. The system could record the violation. It had no representational room for the judgment itself. The report moved into the compliance workflow without the context that would have made sense to anyone on the shop floor.

That episode is one face of a wider structural condition. Matt Vidal's theory of the *management contradiction* (Vidal, 2020, 2022) and Wolfgang Menz and Sarah Nies's analysis of *indirect control* (Menz & Nies, 2024) give me the vocabulary for it. Their central claim, developed in Section 2, is that management has to coordinate worker cooperation for efficiency and discipline that cooperation for *valorisation* (extracting profit from labour) at once. That contradiction sharpens under *postfordism* (today's flexible-production economy, in which scale and standardization no longer drive efficiency on their own) precisely because the flexibility-based gains that matter now depend on the initiative and judgment that discipline is designed to constrain.

When organizations carry that contradiction into AI-driven workplace systems, the result is technology that feels both enabling and coercive. The system I worked with at Darlington is more accurately described as *AI-mediated workflow automation* (rule-based AI that schedules, routes, and triages work along pre-set pathways, not open-ended autonomous AI). That puts it apart from "agentic AI" in the governance-literature sense (Chan et al., 2023; Kellogg et al., 2020). It scheduled inspections, mined incident data for predictive views, routed compliance work across departments, and triaged reports through analytics pipelines and rule-based workflows rather than open-ended autonomous action. I use *bounded intensification* (developed in Section 4) as a working extension of Vidal's (2020, 2022) account to this specific case. The paper's contribution is the empirical demonstration that the dynamic is already active at the automation baseline.

I write this as an autoethnographer, not a case-study researcher. My empirical claims are bounded by where I stood in the organization. They show the mechanisms at work. They do not document them exhaustively. That choice obligates me to be precise about what I saw from my position and to resist the case-study's implicit claim of having surveyed the whole site.

The primary question is *how* AI-mediated cooperation and control come paired. They inherit management's structural contradiction. That contradiction sharpens when automated grammars of action displace tacit managerial satisficing. The setting is bounded (a nuclear refurbishment with unusual safety stakes), so I treat claims about generalization as hypotheses to test, not conclusions already scaled.

In Section 2, I build the theoretical vocabulary: the management contradiction and satisficing (Vidal), and technology as the carrier of the contradiction (Suchman and Agre), with indirect control and legitimation (Menz and Nies) brought in as the regime form. In Section 3, I apply that vocabulary to Darlington, tracing the organizational substrate, two episodes of everyday reconciliation work, and what the case reveals. In Section 4, I develop *bounded intensification* and draw out observation handles for further work. Section 5 concludes.

## 2. The Management Contradiction

Section 2 builds the vocabulary the Darlington analysis requires. Vidal's management contradiction and the satisficing logic that sustains it come first, because they name the structural bind that the rest of the analysis inherits. Suchman and Agre then explain how technology absorbs and operationalizes the contradiction. That mechanism is the one this essay traces at Darlington.

My argument draws on linked concepts. The management contradiction names the structural source. Indirect control describes the regime form it takes on the ground. Bounded intensification is the name I give to what happens when that regime migrates into automated grammars of action. The grammar-practice gap names the boundary where the migration lands. Articulation work names the labour that spans the gap. The legitimation question names why the arrangement persists and where it becomes fragile.

### 2.1 Vidal's Contradiction and Satisficing

Vidal (2020, 2022) identifies two structural contradictions in the capitalist labour process. The first, and the one this essay foregrounds, is the *management contradiction*: a clash between two functions management is expected to perform at once. Managers **coordinate**. They direct, adjust, and align the division of labour so work runs efficiently. Coordination is productive through planning, work design, and the staffing choices that keep work moving. Managers also **discipline**. They secure enough output for surplus extraction. Discipline is unproductive in Vidal's sense when it serves the capital–labour relation rather than the organization of work itself (Vidal, 2020, pp. 187–188).

What matters in Vidal's formulation is that the tension sits *between functions*, not between good and bad outcomes. Earlier labour-process writing, including Gordon et al. (1982), often cast the issue as managers choosing control *over* efficiency. Vidal narrows the claim. The contradiction lives *inside* the pursuit of efficiency. Coordination and discipline both aim at organizational efficiency. Yet they tug managers in opposite directions. Coordination needs worker knowledge, initiative, and cooperation. Discipline needs compliance, predictability, and control. Raising efficiency through coordination means empowering workers with skill and discretion. Securing valorisation through discipline means constraining the very discretion coordination calls for (Vidal, 2020, p. 190).

None of this is brand new. Marx (1990) already read management as both productive (organizing cooperation) and unproductive (enforcing exploitation). Braverman (1974) gave that tension an empirical spine in the twentieth-century division of labor, before Vidal (2020, 2022) narrowed the management contradiction to the clash of functions inside efficiency itself. Under Fordism the tension was easier to live with. Coordination and discipline could ride the same bundle of practices. Mass production with semiskilled labour made technical control efficient and disciplining at once (Vidal, 2020, p. 188).

Under postfordism they pull apart. Efficiency shifts from scale and automation toward scope and flexibility. Advantage rests on quality, speed, and continuous-improvement programs that need workers who can problem-solve and adapt without waiting for a new routing slip. Capital's discipline imperative does not go away. Management still has to secure output, pace, and surplus extraction. The two functions now point in opposite directions. Management becomes, in Vidal's phrase, *divided* (Vidal, 2022, p. 4). The arrangement is not a stable settlement. It is, in Vidal's reading, an inherently contradictory and unstable system the labour process cannot resolve through better management technique alone (Vidal, 2022, pp. 4–6). On Darlington's refurbishment that instability wore safety orange. Program rhetoric demanded both seamless cooperation and non-negotiable discipline, often in the same weekly meeting.

Vidal's second contradiction is the *workforce contradiction*: the friction workers feel between cooperation and the alienation built into a workplace they don't ultimately control. It names the friction workers feel between productive socialization (the upgrading of capabilities through cooperation and engagement with complex systems) and the alienation built into a labour process where management keeps the exclusive right to allocate surplus, veto decisions, and withdraw participation unilaterally (Vidal, 2020, pp. 189, 191–192, 197). That is why worker skepticism in the face of "empowerment" reads as rational rather than recalcitrant. The skepticism I heard on the floor tracked the veto structure long before anyone cited Vidal in my hearing.

How do managers live with the contradiction day to day? Vidal turns to what Simon called *satisficing*[^1]: settling for "good enough" rather than the theoretical best. Pulled to empower for coordination and to discipline for valorisation, managers usually lean toward discipline. Substantive empowerment is hard to build and harder to keep: "So long as key customers are being satisfied, there is not intense pressure to empower workers, even if doing so would increase productivity and flexibility" (Vidal, 2020, p. 195).

Vidal's point is that most managers do not take the empowerment path. The reasons are structural rather than personal. That satisficing yields what Vidal calls the *capitalist production of inefficiency*: "capitalist management is producing inefficiency insofar as it fails to empower workers to engage in intellectual labour (problem-solving and decision-making) or to train them in multiple skills" (Vidal, 2020, p. 194).

Menz and Nies (2024) describe the regime form this contradiction takes in postfordist settings. They call it *indirect control*: managing workers through market-style targets they don't directly control, with managerial problems delegated downward. Workload is no longer justified by Taylorist appeals to merit and scientific calculation but by appeals to market pressure treated as self-evident necessity (Menz & Nies, 2024, pp. 180–182). That shift names what they call the regime's *legitimation* (the case management makes for why an arrangement is fair), and whether that appeal to market facticity holds is what the Darlington episodes test empirically.

### 2.2 Technology as the Carrier of the Contradictory Regime

The theoretical vocabulary is now in place. The management contradiction names the structural source. Indirect control describes the regime form it takes. What remains is how that contradiction reaches the technology workers actually use.

The contradiction reaches technology by being already inscribed in the requirements that build it. Adler and Borys (1996) name the dual valence. The same formal system can feel *enabling* (supporting mastery, transparency, skill) or *coercive* (enforcing compliance, trimming discretion, hoarding knowledge). The tilt is set by design logic. Under Vidal's coordination/discipline pair, the design logic is split. Systems carry the same contradiction into their operating logic. They are asked to augment workers and standardize, watch, and constrain at once. Organizations at odds with themselves buy technology that sits at odds with its users.

The grammar-practice gap that Agre (1995) identifies in workplace software has its intellectual home in Suchman's (2006) foundational study of plans and situated action. Suchman showed that plans and formal procedures are not determinants of action but resources for it: "plans are resources for situated action, but do not in any strong sense determine its course" (p. 72). Action is primarily *situated*, embedded in particular, concrete circumstances that no formal schema can fully capture. When action proceeds smoothly, rules stay invisible. They become explicit only at breakdown, and even then "situated action… is not made explicit by rules and procedures" but is "made accountable to them" (p. 74). Any formal grammar will therefore leave a residue of situated practice that the grammar cannot hold.

Agre (1995) operationalizes this insight in computing. Workplace systems impose *grammars of action*, formal schemes that capture activity as categorical inputs. Workers translate situated practice into those categories. Standardizable events fit cleanly. Judgment, improvisation, and the contextual detail that keeps an incident report honest strain the same fields. The space between grammar and situation is filled by *articulation work* in Star and Strauss's (1999) sense: the labour of knitting together distributed activity and bridging what formal plans miss. That labour stays invisible because the grammar has no words for it. Borrowing Agre's STS-grounded vocabulary and Star and Strauss's CSCW vocabulary into a labour-process register flattens some of their constructionist commitments. I keep the operational readings and acknowledge the trade.

One register-setting clarification before I turn to Darlington. The recent AI-governance literature uses *agentic* as a technical shorthand for stacks that combine autonomy, goal-directedness, and planning under light supervision (Chan et al., 2023; Shavit et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). I follow that literature rather than industry marketing copy. *Algorithmic management* at the other pole recommends and constrains without closing loops on its own (Kellogg et al., 2020). The system I worked with at Darlington does not belong at the agentic pole. It is better described as *AI-mediated workflow automation*: rule-based scheduling, predictive analytics, and compliance routing whose autonomy was bounded by pre-specified pathways rather than open-ended goal pursuit.

That positioning lets me claim something stronger than a future-tense agentic reading would allow. The bounded-intensification dynamics I trace below do *not* require genuinely agentic capability to manifest. They emerge when any sufficiently capable automation layer absorbs managerial satisficing into its grammars while leaving the legitimation question unanswered. Agentic systems would amplify what is already visible here. They are not the minimum condition for it.

## 3. The Contradiction at Work: Darlington

This section brings the theoretical map from Section 2 to one site. Ontario Power Generation's Darlington Nuclear Generating Station sits east of Toronto on Lake Ontario. It is one of the country's largest infrastructure efforts. The refurbishment program replaced four CANDU reactors in sequence, with more than 4,000 people on site at peak. Budzier and Maylor (2025) call the program a "unicorn," a megaproject that met budget and schedule in an industry where most run late and over cost. I start with the organizational substrate in which the AI-mediated Health and Safety Information System sat. Then I describe the system's enabling and coercive faces at the automation baseline. Then I describe the articulation work that spanned the gap between formal grammar and shop-floor practice and what that shows about the case.

### 3.1 The Setting

I draw here on my direct experience inside the program's contracting stack, 2017–2021. Darlington's refurbishment was not a single employer operating a single workforce but a layered contracting stack. Ontario Power Generation sat at the top as owner-licensee, holding the nuclear operating licence and answering to the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission. Beneath OPG, general contractors held the prime construction contracts for each reactor unit; beneath them, trade subcontractors covered specialized work (mechanical, electrical, instrumentation, scaffolding, insulation, rigging), and most workers on the floor received their pay-cheques from those subcontractors. The single-contractor model had collapsed when a pre-requisite facility ran badly over plan. In response, OPG restructured into a multi-party Engineering, Procurement, and Construction Management (EPCM) arrangement. That arrangement carried three commitments: a "Best Athlete" doctrine that pooled fee across firms and staffed positions by competence rather than by badge, a "One Team" overlay that blurred the contractor–client interface at the working level, and a No Surprises Agreement with the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission that bound the program to early disclosure of emerging issues (Budzier & Maylor, 2025).

Beneath those commitments ran the daily reporting architecture I return to in Section 3.2. A simple hours-and-personnel count was submitted to the owner's Board of Directors as a schedule signal. A Level 5 schedule specified daily work packages and watched them at fine grain. A root-cause analysis was opened against every slip from plan (Budzier & Maylor, 2025). Those three surfaces (the count, the schedule, the root-cause file) are the visible face of discipline I lived inside for four years.

The reporting chain ran in two overlapping directions on different clocks. A *functional* chain inside each employer (trade foreman to subcontractor site lead, weekly cadence) ran one way. A *project* chain across employers (every worker scored daily against the integrated work plan that OPG and the general contractors owned jointly) ran the other. The cadence asymmetry mattered. A worker could clear a trade-side weekly review on Friday and arrive at Monday's daily Level 5 read with a fresh project-side variance assembled from numbers collected by parties above them. Reconciliation between the two chains was not anyone's titled job.

My position was Station Condition Record (SCR) Coordinator, a non-union management role inside OPG's Maintenance Strategies and Performance Improvement function. The SCR system itself was the program's heavier-weight corrective-action instrument. Any worker, supervisor, or compliance officer could initiate an SCR against an emergent condition that fell outside routine work-order resolution. The initiation criteria included safety relevance, recurring failure modes, regulatory implication, and any condition that the integrated work plan's existing categories could not absorb. Once initiated, an SCR moved through a governance pathway. The pathway had three stages. It began with the SCR Coordinator's intake review, escalated through a departmental screening committee that decided whether the condition warranted root-cause investigation, and closed with a causes-and-corrective-actions document that named owners and dates for each corrective action. SCR escalation to the Vice-Presidential level was reserved for conditions that crossed departmental boundaries, recurred after prior corrective action had closed, or carried regulatory exposure. In practice, escalation happened roughly once a quarter against the steady-state SCR load. Yet the coordinator role sat against the Level 5 schedule continuously. When a Level 5 variance traced back to an emergent condition rather than to a sequencing slip, an SCR was the instrument that pulled the variance into a structured investigation rather than letting it dissipate into the next week's plan revision. The role had two reporting lines: one functional into performance-improvement, one project line that escalated upward through a departmental Vice President when incident clusters recurred. Alongside the SCR pathway, lower-level diagnostic instruments (operator and supervisor surveys, targeted safety walks) could surface patterns that fed SCR initiation without themselves entering the formal governance chain. Their findings were evidence, not corrective actions.

Management staff are a small minority of the refurbishment workforce. Roughly eighty-eight percent of OPG's regular employees are unionized: about two-thirds through the Power Workers Union, the remainder through the Society of United Professionals. The remaining twelve percent sit as non-union management whose salaries are approved by the Board of Directors rather than negotiated in a collective agreement (Ontario Power Generation, 2020). Management compensation in the refurbishment window was further bounded by Ontario's 2011–2015 base-salary freeze, by the Broader Public Sector Executive Compensation Act's regulation on Vice-Presidents and above after 2019, and by the one-percent moderation period under Bill 124 (Ontario Power Generation, 2020). Those constraints did not reach the floor. They reached me.

Canadian nuclear construction trades are overwhelmingly unionized. Trade unions on Ontario nuclear construction sites are coordinated through the Provincial Building and Construction Trades Council of Ontario. Their collective agreements gave workers real protections: rules over who could do what work, the right to refuse or grieve a directive without being called insubordinate, and the right to stand down on safety grounds that schedule pressure could not override. Any compliance push that ran into those protections had to stop at the union boundary. The German codetermination origin of Menz and Nies's *inner marketization* construct (bringing market pressures inside the company, so internal teams face one another the way buyers face sellers) is worth flagging, because Canadian nuclear construction is not that institutional environment. Even so, the presence of certified trade unions with contractual footholds still bounded what indirect control could get away with at the trade level.

My level was a different story. Non-union management had no union, no grievance process, no work-rule protection. We were the ones who absorbed the gap between what the Level 5 schedule said and what was actually happening on the floor. We did it without any contractual friction to slow that down. That is not a weakness in the account that follows. It is the closest available view from inside the regime this essay describes.

My standpoint shapes what this account can and cannot claim. From the management-system interface I had direct access to several layers. These included the Health and Safety Information System's design logic, its classification rules and routing pathways, the compliance metrics and dashboard readings that senior leadership relied on, and the second-order patterns visible from working through the corrective-action workflow across the program. For escalated SCR investigations (at Level 5) the coordinator role required local surveys and direct observation alongside subject-matter experts (SME) to identify root causes. That brought me onto the floor alongside the workers involved. That access was episodic and investigation-framed rather than continuous.

One example shows what that access could find that system traces could not. Workers in the turbine area were reporting hand injuries: not once, not twice, but as a recurring pattern. The procedure was unambiguous. When we reviewed the records, every box had been checked. When we asked the workers, they insisted they had done everything right. The system agreed with them. And the injuries kept coming. So we went to the floor. We watched the work happen in real time. For a while, nothing looked wrong. The workers moved through the task the way any trained crew would, following the sequence, hitting the marks. And then, without announcement or apparent decision, the gloves came off.

They were not cutting corners. They were not even aware they were doing it. Only once you are standing in a turbine area on a hot summer afternoon, where the ambient temperature can reach fifty degrees Celsius, does the physiology become self-evident. The body sheds the gloves the way it sheds any unbearable layer. The workers had not connected that automatic response to the injuries, and neither had the system, because the system had no way to record what sustained heat does to a person mid-task. The fix was straightforward once the root cause was visible: replace the standard gloves with heat-resistant alternatives. The recurrence stopped. That finding lived nowhere in the system. It required someone standing on the floor to see it.

What I did not have is the routine standpoint of a floor-level observer. I did not have the daily texture of accountability pressure as workers felt it, the affective dimensions of constraint, or the informal strategies workers developed outside a formal investigation. Where this account attributes routine experience or response to workers, the basis is inference from system-level traces (SCR patterns, survey results, count-versus-practice divergences) rather than direct observation of everyday worker subjectivity. The episodes that follow are reconstructed from my recollection of the work. The actual operational documents that traced back to specific incidents cannot be reproduced.

From 2017 to 2021 I worked with an AI-mediated Health and Safety Information System at Darlington, linking analytics sprints to life on a nuclear construction site. The system was not an autonomous agent. It was AI-mediated workflow automation. It scheduled inspections along pre-specified calendars. It mined incident data for predictive views. It routed compliance work across departments along rule-based paths. It triaged incoming reports against fixed taxonomies with limited per-action human steering. That is more than a passive database. It is a system that moves on organizational data with little hands-on input for each step, while remaining firmly inside the pathways it was built to follow.

Budzier and Maylor (2025) document the same site's structures from program documents and senior interviews. They do not trace the invisible labour that kept the program moving. I write from the seam between that stack and the shift, in my own words and without copying language or identifiers from employer systems. People are left unnamed where the point is structural. Operational specifics that would read as a fresh secret have been trimmed. What remains should be what you could already piece together from Budzier and Maylor's published account of the program, plus what I can fairly say I remember from having lived inside that work.

### 3.2 Enabling and Coercive Faces

With the contracting stack and reporting architecture in place, two episodes show how the management contradiction surfaced as everyday reconciliation work at the boundary between the system's metric grammar (Agre, 1995) and situated practice. I want to give the enabling side its due before turning to constraints. The system really did enable cooperation that would have been hard to sustain otherwise. Predictive views surfaced patterns across thousands of reports that no single officer could hold in view. Cross-department coordination, once carried by meetings, calls, and paper, ran through algorithmic routing. Information moved in near real time, so workers and managers could see safety conditions across the site.

Together with the "One Team" and "Best Athlete" arrangements named in Section 3.1, that infrastructure tracked what Vidal names as coordination: management's productive face. I felt that side of the system when it let me see trends across departments, raise risks early, and stitch responses among teams that might have stayed in silos.

The same system also constrained. Fixed incident categories asked workers to fit events into labels that often misrepresented what happened. Compliance officers acted as gates. Data rules defined who saw what, who could relabel, and who could pull aggregate analytics. The control infrastructure already named in Section 3.1 built a long accountability trail.

The next two episodes show what indirect control looked like on the ground. Each time, a number the system demanded met the reality of actual work, the two did not fit, and someone ended up bridging the gap by hand.

**Episode 1: Observation-and-Coaching target counts against situated quality.** The Observation and Coaching (O&C) program was one of the tools the site used to keep safety behaviour in check. Supervisors and peers each had a quota of field observations to complete every period. For each one, they recorded what they saw against a fixed list of categories (whether the worker followed the procedure, wore the right protective equipment, kept the area tidy, and moved safely), and then had a brief coaching conversation with the worker. Those four categories covered the most common issues. They could not capture the full complexity of what actually happens on a multi-trade nuclear construction floor: things like work sequencing, who had access to which area, or the mental load of managing competing tasks at once. What showed up on the dashboard was a simple count: how many observations each supervisor and each area had logged that week. Those numbers rolled up into quarterly scorecards and fed into the safety column of the integrated project schedule.

The situated reality the count failed to hold was quality. Over roughly two and a half years, the site accumulated 458 O&C records that, read end to end, told a uniform story of compliance against a story the underlying incident data did not corroborate. The same reporting window produced 100 Station Condition Records (the site's heavier-weight corrective-action instrument) and a 220-respondent operator-and-supervisor diagnostic survey run through the program's lower-level safety assurance pathway. Read together, that evidence revealed a *structural dislocation* (a gap between what a metric measures and what actually happens on the ground) at the seam: the pattern at target-hit and the pattern at shop-floor reality had been pulling apart for two and a half years, silently. Yet the 458 records said supervisors were observing diligently. The 100 SCRs and the survey said the observations were not changing what the work actually looked like.

I prepared the SCR investigation that pulled those three data sources into a single view (what Menz and Nies [2024, pp. 180–182] call the fraying of market-facticity legitimation under indirect control). Drawing the survey's findings into the formal governance pathway was itself the escalation. It ran for several months, moved through formal governance, and closed with a causes-and-corrective-actions document that senior leadership endorsed. One of its findings was that the organization was putting more emphasis on meeting O&C target numbers than on the observational quality those numbers were meant to proxy. That is the empowerment-and-measurement dynamic Agre (1995) traces in Section 2.2: supervisors empowered to observe, paired with measurement that disciplines the observer. Supervisors were activated as observing subjects, the count was rhetorically tied to "empowerment," and the merit appeal (effort to observe was supposed to translate into safer practice) was the legitimation principle fraying at the seam. Effort tied to a count, not to outcomes the count cannot represent. The count was clean. The practice beneath the count was not.

The SCR investigation, including the survey that prompted it, was itself a Suchman (2006) moment. The O&C rules had been invisible so long as practice approximately tracked the count. At breakdown, when the gap became undeniable, the rules were "explicated for purposes of deliberation" and action was made "accountable to them" (p. 74). That accountability ran retrospectively. The goal was to close the gap, not to revise what the count measured.

The organizational consequence followed the pattern the paper is trying to name. Corrective actions were written, assigned, tracked, and in time closed. Whether the revised targets actually changed supervisor behaviour on the floor, or whether the new quality fields were themselves re-optimized against the next quarter's scorecards, was a question the investigation tracked to action closure rather than outcome verification. Its accountability ran to whether corrective actions had been closed, not to whether the seam had actually narrowed. The survey, a diagnostic instrument that had named the gap before the governance response ran, was not positioned to answer once the SCR pathway took over. What the site counted after the investigation was whether the corrective actions had been closed. The question of whether the seam between target-hit and observational-quality had actually narrowed was not a field in the system.

The turbine investigation in Section 3.1 is the contrast: standing on the floor identified a situated cause, and the fix was a different glove. That path was available because the cause was physical. A metric grammar has no equivalent. The accountability ran to closing the corrective actions, not to revising what the count was designed to see.

That is the bounded-intensification pattern at the O&C scale. A metric could not hold the quality of what it was measuring, over two and a half years. The reconciliation burden fell to an investigation that had no field in the system.

The shape of that first episode is what Adler and Borys (1996) would read as the coercive face of a system that, in other moments, genuinely enabled. The metric did what the metric was designed to do. Its field definitions closed down faster than the situated reality they were meant to describe. The articulation work I supplied (Star & Strauss, 1999) was necessary for the dashboard to mean what senior leadership read it as meaning. That labour had no line item.

**Episode 2: The daily count as a lived reconciliation cycle.** Episode 2 lives at a different timescale than Episode 1. Where Episode 1 ran across two and a half years and closed with a multi-month investigation, Episode 2 ran across a single working day and repeated every working day for the duration of execution. The daily count's accountability runs to a market-style schedule signal a worker has no leverage over, and its legitimation is engineered to read as the necessary face of the program's commitment to the province. Specifically, Budzier and Maylor (2025) document the architecture. The refurbishment submitted a daily report to OPG's Board of Directors that was read as a schedule signal. Daily production targets were scored in points against a Level 5 schedule that specified daily work packages. Every deviation from the Level 5 daily plan triggered a root-cause analysis.

The fields the daily count actually contained were spare. They tracked personnel on site by trade and by contractor, hours worked against hours scheduled, work-package status flags coded against the Level 5 plan, and a free-text variance field for any cell that did not resolve cleanly into one of the closed-form codes. The supervisor who closed out a shift made coding decisions on the spot for any worker whose day did not fit the closed forms. An "absence" code applied when a worker booked off. A "re-deployment" code applied when a worker was pulled from one work package to another inside the same shift. The two codes had different downstream consequences. An absence widened the trade's ratio of hours-scheduled to hours-worked and showed up on the next morning's Board read as a productivity dip. A re-deployment kept the trade's ratio intact but showed up as a Level 5 variance against the work package the worker had been pulled away from.

Inside that field structure, the Level 5 variance-tolerance threshold operated as the gate between routine reconciliation and root-cause escalation. The threshold was not a fixed percentage but a working judgment the program managers held about how much daily variance could be absorbed into the weekly schedule revision without escalating to a formal root-cause file. A variance inside tolerance closed quietly into the next week's plan. A variance outside tolerance opened a root-cause analysis the SCR Coordinator function would track to closure.

What the mechanism reconciled was the gap between what the program promised the province (delivery on schedule and on budget) and the distributed, asynchronous practice of a site organized by reporting chains that ran in two overlapping directions. The reconciliation had to happen daily because the Board was reading the count daily. The count had to mean something when it was read. This is the structural dislocation at the seam made routine: a metric grammar engineered to be legible upward. It demanded continuous repair downward. No role title admitted the repair was happening.

That is the bounded-intensification pattern at the architectural scale. A number engineered to be legible upward demanded continuous, unnamed repair beneath. The architecture provided no role title for the repair.

The two episodes together show the pattern is a property of the seam rather than of any single metric. Episode 1 was a number that could not hold the quality of what it was counting, surfaced over two and a half years. Episode 2 is a number built simple enough for a Board to read, demanding continuous repair beneath to remain legible as a schedule signal. The point is not that such gaps happened *occasionally* but that the relationship between dashboard and floor required continuous, unrecorded reconciliation to hold. Vidal's discipline pole and Menz and Nies's indirect-control regime are not separate phenomena here. They are the same arrangement seen from different angles, where metrics, compliance records, schedule scores, and root-cause files form the visible face of control while judgment and improvisation supply the invisible layer on which the enabling story depended.

### 3.3 Articulation Work and What the Case Reveals

The two episodes above show the contradiction at the metric grammar level. What this section traces is what it costs at the human level: the articulation work the grammar cannot hold.

Vidal's (2020) workforce contradiction surfaced on site as the asymmetry the empowerment talk could not hide. A crew or compliance officer could surface a sequencing fix through the continuous-improvement loop and watch it travel up into the lessons-learned record. The decision that mattered, whether a scope change could actually be authorized under the minimum-scope decision tree, or whether the Level 5 schedule owner would absorb the variance, came back down from authorities the proposer could not see. As Vidal writes, "Management retains the exclusive right to allocate the surplus produced, along with veto power, one-way channels of communication, and the power to withdraw participation rights unilaterally" (Vidal, 2020, p. 189). Reticence followed. People cooperated within bounds they trusted but did not invest in initiatives they understood as partial and revocable, because experience already showed that empowerment without authority adds responsibility without protection. The reconciliation work therefore fell to intermediaries like me rather than to distributed initiative on the shop floor.

I did that work daily. The SCR Coordinator role carried a steady-state load of roughly fifteen to twenty active SCRs at any given time, ranging from intake-screening to final causes-and-corrective-actions closure. Across a typical weekday, category-reconciliation work (the small re-codings, field re-labellings, and conversations with compliance officers about which taxonomy a particular observation belonged in) ran at roughly two to three hours. Root-cause-analysis writing for variances that had crossed the prior shift's tolerance threshold ran at another two to three hours. Cross-departmental coordination calls took another hour or two. The remainder went to the SCR governance pathway itself. Governance reviews ran on a weekly cadence (a department-level scorecard read on Tuesday afternoon and a cross-departmental coordination call on Thursday morning), with a higher-altitude monthly review feeding into the quarterly leadership read. None of these categories was named in any role description. They were how I distributed the work that arrived.

The physical artefacts of that cadence matter as much as the time-budget. A typical weekday had four to six screens open simultaneously: the SCR system, the Level 5 schedule view, the safety dashboard, and a coordination window for cross-departmental threads. Paper artefacts moved through the role at a steadier cadence than any digital one would suggest. Handover sheets passed across shift changes carried tacit knowledge the digital systems could not store: the supervisor's read of which crew was running long, which coordination call had not yet landed a commitment, which root-cause-analysis file was waiting on a witness statement. The five-minute conversation between the outgoing and incoming SCR Coordinator (the verbal pass of "watch this one, the screening committee deferred it pending more data") was the load-bearing knowledge transfer the systems could not capture.

That cadence and those artefacts are what indirect control looked like at the boundary between the system's grammar of action (Agre, 1995) and situated practice in a 2018–2019 nuclear refurbishment that was, by all the measures the Board read, delivering on schedule. Nothing about the cadence was scripted. Nothing about the artefacts was documented as required infrastructure. Yet the program's daily Board read was unintelligible without both. The hours spent deciding whether a deviation was "absence" or "re-deployment" made the next morning's count mean what it was read as meaning. The handover sheets carried tacit knowledge across shift boundaries the digital systems would have lost. The cross-departmental coordination calls pulled Level 5 variances back inside tolerance before the weekly review.

Vidal's discipline pole and Menz and Nies's indirect-control regime are not abstractions when they meet a working day. They are the four-screen weekday, the paper handover sheet, and the Thursday-morning coordination call. The articulation work that converts those particulars into a Board-readable count is the labour the management contradiction is offloading onto the people standing at the grammar-practice boundary.

I moved between the system's grammar and workers' situated knowledge: forcing incidents into categories they resisted, reading predictive signals against context the model never saw, holding compliance demands alongside judgments the procedures could not store. Star and Strauss (1999) call this articulation work: the continuous job of knitting distributed activity and handling what formal plans miss. I borrow that descriptive apparatus from a CSCW register into a labour-process register, keeping the operational reading and acknowledging the trade. Under the management contradiction, articulation becomes something sharper than contingency management. It is navigation through incoherence. The system asked for compliance and initiative at once, and for a standardized grammar and judgment that grammar could not hold. Enabling and coercive pressures met on workers and intermediaries like me.

That labour stayed off the books. Safety metrics, schedule packs, and compliance views did not track it. In Agre's terms, the grammar of action captured what standardized: counts, categories, response times, completion rates (Agre, 1995). It had no field for the judgment that made those figures honest. Naming the labour would have exposed how cooperation on screen leaned on uncounted effort to span coercive rails, so it rarely surfaced. Secrecy was not the issue. The grammar had no line item for it, and the organization had little reason to invent one. Even Budzier and Maylor (2025), on the same site with hundreds of documents and senior interviews, do not foreground this work; their account covers delivery, safety, and culture without unpacking the invisible stitching, which is itself a sign of how structural invisibility can persist for careful observers.

Darlington illustrates the theory inside the boundary conditions of a single nuclear refurbishment program. What I wrestled with looked like Vidal's (2020, 2022) management contradiction in safety clothing: cooperation and compliance both had to advance. Without Vidal's frame, the same field notes get filed as "change management" or "user adoption." The enabling and coercive faces, the workforce skepticism, and the off-books bridging all line up with the structure of the contradiction. They line up more cleanly with the contradiction than with any one design choice or bad call by a manager. The tension belongs to the labour process. It does not end when a rollout ends.

The case, in turn, sharpens the theory in one respect. Nuclear safety pushes both coordination and discipline toward their limits. Relaxing coordination would endanger workers under the regulatory and radiological facts of the site. Relaxing discipline would endanger the public downstream of the plant. In most settings a manager might satisfice by quietly letting one pole weaken. At Darlington the stakes foreclosed that path. Both functions stayed non-negotiable, so the contradiction surfaced with unusual clarity rather than dissolving into routine trade-offs. The three subsections above have traced that dynamic at different scales (the O&C pattern over two and a half years, the daily count cycle, and the coordinator cadence). The Darlington case now grounds the *bounded intensification* reading that Section 4 develops.

## 4. Discussion

The discussion has two moves. I develop *bounded intensification* at the automation baseline and use the Darlington material in Section 3 to show why cooperation and control travel together when managerial satisficing migrates into rule-based workflow automation. I then read the case as a window onto how the contradictory regime stays unstable under indirect control. That second move asks three things: where the legitimation question surfaces, what reconciliation work the architecture relies on without naming, and what concrete observation handles fall out of taking that reading seriously.

### 4.1 Bounded Intensification and Systemic Inefficiency

The Darlington case supports the *bounded intensification* reading introduced in Section 1. AI-mediated workflow automation absorbs managerial satisficing into computational routines while leaving the underlying contradiction intact. The practical weight the contradiction lays on workers and intermediaries at the grammar-practice boundary then rises. Not because the system has become agentic, but because fewer human pauses remain to hold the tension. The Tuesday morning incident that opens this essay is the same displacement at minimum scale. The two episodes in Section 3 demonstrate it at institutional and architectural timescales.

The mechanism is specific. Vidal's satisficing account explains why managers lean toward discipline. It is lighter lift, and the contradiction makes deep empowerment costly. What the account does not address is what happens when satisficing migrates from a manager's ongoing situational judgment into the rule base. A manager can revise a judgment under pressure, reading context and noticing when a number fails to capture what happened. An automated routine asserts its rule and moves on. The reconciliation that a manager might once have absorbed now falls to whoever stands at the grammar-practice boundary. That is the displacement *bounded intensification* names. It is fully visible at the automation baseline without requiring any genuinely agentic capability.

The O&C investigation showed the pattern at an institutional timescale. A count said one thing. Survey and corrective-action data said another. A months-long reconciliation pulled those readings into a single finding the system could register. The same shape runs at smaller scale. The micro-sequence across a single supervisor's afternoon (a category that did not hold, a judgment that exceeded the field, an hour spent forcing the mismatch back into workflow) is the same pattern run once.

That pattern activates and clips worker and intermediary subjectivity (Menz & Nies, 2024). Articulation work absorbs the gap between grammar and floor (Star & Strauss, 1999).

The bounded-intensification reading sharpens against the EPCM architecture. A multi-party stack designed for prerequisite-failure resilience generated coordination demands the daily count could not absorb.

Grammars of action reach further into practice, as Agre (1995) would expect. More capture tends to require more articulation to bridge grammar and situation, and that bridging is often less visible in the formal governance record. Accountability thins where Shavit et al. (2023) locate widening governance gaps. Workers can carry blame for outcomes they do not steer, the pattern Elish (2019) calls *moral crumple zones*.

Vidal's sharper claim is that satisficing under the contradiction yields *systemic inefficiency*: "capitalist management is producing inefficiency insofar as it fails to empower workers to engage in intellectual labour (problem-solving and decision-making) or to train them in multiple skills" (Vidal, 2020, p. 194). Firms that truly empower tend to outperform. Yet most stop short, because satisficing is lighter lift and the contradictions make deep empowerment costly.

Darlington makes that point concrete. Safety could have gained from richer respect for situated judgment. The discipline side limited that path. Automated stacks default toward what is easy to discipline. They optimize for what fits the dashboard and systematically discount what does not.

### 4.2 Observation Handles for the Unstable Regime

Reading Darlington as a case of an unstable indirect-control regime turns the legitimation question into an empirical one and surfaces concrete handles for further work. The most concrete is an empirical program for measuring articulation work, extending Star and Strauss's (1999) argument that the invisibility of work is itself an analytic object. Budzier and Maylor (2025) document that the program already measured what it wanted to optimize: a Level 5 schedule, a daily productivity count submitted to the owner's Board, and a root-cause file opened against every slip from plan. What it did not measure was the reconciliation work that kept those numbers legible. The empirical program above would treat the four-screen weekday and the shift-change handover sheet as countable artefacts, not as anecdote, asking per role and per week how the second underwrites the first.

Three further handles fall out of the same reading. The legitimation question is the most tractable empirically. Where supervisors and workers locate the appeal that justifies a target, and what happens when that appeal frays, is a survey-and-interview object that high-reliability research has not yet treated as such. Requirements analysts working on workflow-automation procurement face a prior design choice. Do grammars carry durable fields for contested judgment, or do they route every exception as a data-quality issue? That decision predetermines how much articulation work the procurement will silently transfer onto whoever stands at the grammar-practice boundary. For procurement offices and labour advocates, indirect-control vocabulary offers a more precise instrument than current disclosure frameworks provide. It names dashboards that rebrand discipline as empowerment without resourcing the intermediary roles those dashboards depend on. Each handle is partial. Together they make the costs of the contradictory regime harder to naturalize as background noise.

The baseline itself is the essay's contribution. Bounded intensification names what AI-mediated workflow automation already does to Vidal's (2020, 2022) management contradiction, before any question about agentic systems arrives. The handles above do not depend on where the capability frontier settles next. They depend on what the organization already measures and what it still refuses to measure.

I end with questions rather than a closing verdict because premature closure is exactly what a labour-process account should refuse. If the contradiction is structural and tightens with automation, several questions stay open. Can AI-mediated systems name both coordination and discipline plainly instead of dressing control as cooperation? What changes if articulation work, today invisible, shows up in metrics, dashboards, and design reviews? At Darlington, had the hours I spent reconciling categories with situated judgment appeared in the compliance dashboard alongside response times and completion rates, would management have invested in a more flexible grammar, or would the new line item simply have been optimized into another target? Does visibility ease the contradiction or only move it? When the satisficing actor is the stack rather than a manager, and "good enough" tilts toward discipline because empowerment is harder to compute, who pays? The critical mandate of this work is that the answer to that question belongs in the ledger, not as a design problem to be engineered away, but as labour that has always already been there.

These questions are central to whatever program of work continues after this essay. Design choices not taken here mark the natural extensions. Those extensions include multi-site comparison, quantitative measurement of articulation hours, application to non-nuclear sectors where the discipline pole is configured differently, and explicit tracking of what genuinely agentic systems add on top of the automation baseline.

## 5. Conclusion

I have told this story in the first person to keep visible *how* I came to believe what I am claiming. Management remains structurally pulled in two directions. It coordinates for efficiency and disciplines for valorisation, and these are hard to fuse cleanly. Postfordism sharpens the bind because efficiency now often needs the empowerment discipline works against. Managers satisfice. They settle for control regimes that secure output without fully unlocking creativity. Vidal's capitalist production of inefficiency follows. Organizations fall short of their potential more because of structural conditions that make deep empowerment rare than because individual executives fail in isolation.

Baked into AI-mediated workflow automation, that contradiction passes to the tools. Under indirect control the pairing is often legitimated as market necessity rather than employment structure. Workers span the gap through articulation work that stays off the books. At Darlington I met that pattern every day. The safety information system I worked with opened cooperation that would have been harder without it. It also narrowed situated knowledge, hardened hierarchy, and shaped who saw what. The labour of bridging those faces never hit a dashboard or a job description. I read it now as the likely price of a structural bind no single design choice can absorb.

The bounded-intensification reading advanced here is a claim about the automation baseline Darlington already instantiates. It does not wait for any capability frontier to shift. The contradiction shows up in the architecture the program had in 2020. Four pieces did the work: a daily productivity tally the owner's Board read as a schedule signal, a Level 5 schedule that specified daily work packages, a minimum-scope decision tree that fixed how changes could be judged, and a taxonomy for events that closed down faster than the practice it was meant to describe. Refusing the contradiction, rather than managing its symptoms, would mean letting the reconciliation work that made those categories legible enter the same ledger as the categories themselves. That is a political question about what the organization is willing to count, not a design question about which field to add.

This essay reframes a durable structural bind rather than treating it as a wiring bug with a single patch. It offers words for spotting the tension when it appears and a frame for why AI-mediated workplaces feel the pulls they do. Writing it gave me terms for what I already sensed on site: cooperative and controlling at once because the commissioning organization was already split, not because the builders were uniquely careless.

The incident report from that Tuesday morning is still in the compliance queue: correctly flagged, correctly routed, the judgment that made it make sense absorbed by whoever stood at the seam. The turbine fix is in the corrective-action record too. That finding also lived nowhere in the system until someone went to the floor to look for it. The grammar processed both. It held neither. Whether naming that seam, and measuring the cost of standing in it, can change what organizations are willing to count is the question this essay leaves open. It is the one a labour-process account of AI-mediated work cannot responsibly close. The cost of this structural bind, the hours of reconciliation work the architecture makes invisible, is not an artifact to be fixed. It is a necessary component of the ledger itself, and making it count is the political work this field still owes.
