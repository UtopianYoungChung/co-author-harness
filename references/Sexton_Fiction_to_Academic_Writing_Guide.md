# From Fiction to Academic Writing: Extracted Principles (Adam Sexton, *Master Class in Fiction Writing*)

This document distils instructions from *Master Class in Fiction Writing* (Adam Sexton, McGraw-Hill) and adapts them for **impactful academic papers** (e.g., requirements engineering, CAiSE-style submissions). The source is about fiction; the adaptations aim to keep scholarly standards while improving clarity, engagement, and persuasiveness.

---

## 1. Structure and “Story”

### Fiction principle
- A narrative needs a **central need** (concrete and specific, not abstract), **conflict** (resistance to satisfying that need), **development** (escalating attempts), **climax** (resolution), and optional **denouement** (consequences).
- Open with a **scene** (something the reader can “see”) rather than only exposition; delay heavy exposition until the reader is engaged.
- “Conflict is story.” Without a clear need and obstacles, there is no forward drive.

### Academic adaptation
- **Give the paper one clear “need”**: e.g., “How can we represent identity in RE?” or “What requirements does identity-sensitive modeling make explicit?” State it early and concretely (RQ1, RQ2).
- **Create forward drive**: Present a **gap** (what existing methods do not do), then **development** (your approach, steps, mapping), then **resolution** (answers to the RQs, illustrated by the case).
- **Open with impact, not only context**: After a short setup, move quickly to the problem or a vivid example (e.g., “In a bank’s talent acquisition program, AI screening initially threatened recruiters’ expert-judgment identity…”). Avoid pages of pure background before the reader knows why it matters.
- **Resolve the conflict**: The conclusion should clearly state how the RQs were addressed; avoid ending on vague “future work” without a crisp summary of what was shown.

## 2. Show, Don’t Just Tell (Concrete vs Abstract)

### Fiction principle
- “The best descriptive writing is **concrete**” (experienceable via the senses) and **specific**. “Frightened” is abstract; “eyes squeezed shut, teeth chattering” is concrete.
- **Show, then tell**: Earn the right to abstract claims by preceding them with concrete evidence or examples. “Show *and* tell.”
- Prefer the **intimate senses** (smell, touch, taste) when possible; they bring the reader “onstage.”

### Academic adaptation
- **Ground claims in evidence**: Before stating that “identity concerns arise during adoption,” cite or summarise concrete cases (e.g., bank recruiters, loan consultants, customer-support agents). One strong example often beats a long abstract sentence.
- **One concrete illustration per major claim**: For each important claim (e.g., “person-based identity differs from role-based”), give a short, specific illustration (e.g., Tim vs his Hiring Expert role).
- **Avoid long runs of unsupported abstraction**: Alternate between “what we argue” and “as in the Santander case…” or “e.g., Strich et al. show….”

## 3. Description: Concrete, Specific, Original, Careful

### Fiction principle
- **Concrete**: Can be seen, heard, touched; avoid *only* abstract labels.
- **Specific**: Prefer precise nouns and strong **action verbs** over stacking modifiers (e.g., “trudged” vs “walked slowly and disconsolately”). “Nouns and verbs ought to drive your writing.”
- **Original**: Shun clichés and stock phrases; fresh comparisons stick.
- **Careful**: Observe closely; the best prose comes from precise observation.

### Academic adaptation
- **Precise verbs**: Prefer “demonstrates,” “enables,” “constrains,” “captures” over vague “addresses,” “looks at,” “deals with.” Use one strong verb instead of a weak verb plus adverbs.
- **Specific terms**: Name the method (e.g., “contribution analysis,” “Strategic Dependency model”) rather than only “our approach.” Name the requirement (“Decision Authority be Maintained”) rather than only “authority preservation.”
- **Original phrasing**: Rephrase common phrases in your own words; avoid boilerplate (“In today’s rapidly evolving landscape…”). Define technical terms when first used.
- **Careful use of sources**: Describe what a cited work actually does or shows; avoid generic “X discusses identity.”

## 4. Cause and Effect (Plot)

### Fiction principle
- **Plot = causality**: “The king died and then the queen died” is episodic; “The king died, and then the queen died **of grief**” is plot. One event should lead to the next; avoid arbitrary sequence.
- **No deus ex machina**: Resolutions should follow from what went before, not from an external fix. “Unlikely events can create complications; they shouldn’t untangle them.”

### Academic adaptation
- **Explicit logical links**: Use “therefore,” “thus,” “as a result,” “because” to connect premises to conclusions. Make the argument chain visible (e.g., “Because i* distinguishes agents and roles, we can map person-based identity to agents…”).
- **Avoid unexplained jumps**: If the design follows from theory, say so. If a requirement “emerges” from the model, show the step (e.g., “This softgoal addresses authority ambiguity because…”).
- **No unmotivated solutions**: Design choices (e.g., reciprocal dependencies, “AI Team Capability be Developed”) should be justified by the preceding analysis, not dropped in as fixes.

## 5. Dialogue and “Voice” (Diction and Syntax)

### Fiction principle
- **Diction** (word choice) and **syntax** (sentence structure) define voice. Avoid every character sounding like the author; avoid melodrama (characters saying exactly what they feel).
- **Listen, then write**: Capture how people really speak (fragments, contractions, subtext); avoid long, polished monologues unless character-specific.
- **Imply rather than tag**: Prefer “he said” and let the quoted content carry tone; avoid “he said sarcastically.”

### Academic adaptation
- **Consistent scholarly voice**: Choose a level of formality and stick to it (e.g., “This paper shows” vs “We show”). Prefer active voice and first-person plural where appropriate (“We map…”, “Our analysis demonstrates…”).
- **Vary sentence length and structure**: Mix short, direct sentences with longer, qualified ones. Avoid a long run of similar “This X does Y. This A does B.”
- **Avoid melodrama**: Don’t overstate (“revolutionary,” “completely changes”); let evidence carry weight. Understatement often reads as more credible.

## 6. Title and Opening

### Fiction principle
- A good **title** “intrigues and motivates” someone to begin. Don’t leave work “Untitled”; relate the title to content (central image, question, or idea).
- **Opening**: Favor a scene or a hook over pure exposition. “If you begin with a scene… you can always follow with the necessary exposition.” Delay dull-but-necessary information until the reader is hooked.

### Academic adaptation
- **Title**: Be specific and informative (e.g., “Modeling and Analyzing Identity-Sensitive Requirements in Human-AI Collaboration”). Avoid overly generic titles (“A Study of Requirements”).
- **Abstract and first paragraph**: State the problem and the paper’s contribution early. Use a concrete scenario or a striking gap (e.g., “Current RE practice models stakeholders only through functional roles…”) so the reader quickly sees why the paper matters.
- **Roadmap**: After the contribution, a short “Section 2 does X, Section 3 does Y” helps the reader commit; it’s the academic equivalent of “we read on” to regain balance.

## 7. Planning and Revision

### Fiction principle
- **Short stories**: First draft can be exploratory; then revise for structure (one protagonist, one clear need, development, climax).
- **Novels**: “A book-length story needs planning.” Use a brief, tentative outline (protagonist, need, attempts, outcome); allow discovery but keep the goal in mind to avoid losing the forest in the trees.
- **Development must be sufficient**: “Aim to write stories that are 80 percent development.” A too-short middle yields a feeble climax.

### Academic adaptation
- **Outline before drafting**: Decide main sections (motivation, related work, theory, integration, example, discussion, conclusion) and one-sentence purpose per section. Keep RQs and contribution statement in view.
- **Revise for argument, then style**: First pass—does every paragraph advance the argument? Second pass—sentence length, concrete examples, precise verbs.
- **Give the “middle” its due**: The core of the paper (mapping, procedure, illustrative example) should be substantial. A thin middle and a long “future work” section can make the climax (answers to RQs) feel under-supported.

## 8. Theme and “Message”

### Fiction principle
- “In the absence of story structure, fiction writers lack an effective means to deliver their messages.” Theme is delivered *through* structure and concrete story; “money can’t buy happiness” is just an aphorism until we see it in a character’s life.
- Don’t put “the cart of theme before the horse that is story.”

### Academic adaptation
- **Contribution through structure**: The “message” (e.g., “identity deserves systematic attention in RE”) is convincing when delivered through clear RQs, theory, mapping, and a worked example—not only stated in the introduction.
- **One clear takeaway**: By the end, the reader should be able to state in one sentence what the paper adds (e.g., “a mapping from identity dimensions to i* constructs and a demonstration that it surfaces requirements that conventional analysis does not”).

## 9. Learning from Others

### Fiction principle
- “Writers do not read for fun” (Irving)—read as a writer: for **solutions to problems of craft**. “Read critically, the way one athlete will watch another’s performance” (Mailer).
- Learn by **example**: Study how published stories handle structure, description, dialogue, POV. “Steal” (be inspired by) structure, style, and technique—don’t plagiarise.

### Academic adaptation
- **Read for craft**: In addition to content, notice how strong papers open, transition, use citations, and structure the related work and discussion. Note sentence length and use of examples.
- **Use exemplars**: Keep 1–2 papers you admire (same venue or style) open while drafting. Mimic their section balance, abstract shape, and conclusion structure, not their content.

## 10. Checklist for an Impactful Academic Paper (Sexton-Inspired)

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| **One clear “need”** | RQs stated early; paper resolves them explicitly. |
| **Open with impact** | Problem or concrete example in first 1–2 paragraphs. |
| **Show then tell** | Prefer concrete examples + one clear claim over long abstraction. |
| **Concrete and specific** | Named methods, requirements, and sources; strong verbs. |
| **Cause and effect** | Explicit “therefore,” “because”; design choices motivated. |
| **Consistent voice** | Same level of formality; mix sentence length. |
| **Title and roadmap** | Informative title; short section roadmap after contribution. |
| **Sufficient development** | Core (theory, mapping, example) substantial; climax (answers) clearly stated. |
| **Theme through structure** | Main message emerges from the argument, not only the intro/conclusion. |
| **Revise structure then style** | First: argument and flow; second: sentences and examples. |
