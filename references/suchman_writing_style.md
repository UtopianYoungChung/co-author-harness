## 1. Overview: what the Suchman register sounds like

Suchman's academic prose is characterized by three overarching qualities that distinguish it from generic scholarly writing:

**Interlocutory address.** She writes as though in active dialogue with a named position, not merely surveying a literature. Every section implicitly answers a prior speaker. This produces a voice that is simultaneously humble (she is engaging, not pronouncing) and precise (she knows exactly what position she is refusing).

**Hedged-but-committed first person.** She uses *I* at moments of genuine argumentative commitment, not as throat-clearing or courtesy. When she says "I want to suggest," the suggestion is substantive. The hedge is epistemic precision — it signals the exact scope of the claim — not rhetorical modesty.

**Configuration over causation.** Her descriptions resist simple agent-action-outcome structures when the phenomenon she is describing is distributed across humans, artifacts, and situations. She uses passive constructions and process nominalizations deliberately, to represent distributed production rather than to avoid accountability. This is the opposite of the passive-as-weakness that Bacon warns against.

## 2. The seven characteristic sentence moves

### Move 1 — Interlocutory pivot

**Pattern.** Name the received view or prior claim precisely; then introduce a constraint or limit using "what X cannot account for," "what becomes visible when," or "the question X leaves open."

**Why it matters.** The move positions Suchman's argument as a response to a real position, not a gap in a field. It also performs the Baird "assumption-challenging" mode: she is not gap-spotting but showing where a prior framework's commitments break down.

**Signature phrase types:**
- "What this account cannot accommodate is…"
- "The question that remains is not whether X but how…"
- "What becomes visible when we look closely at X is…"
- "The problem with X is not that it is wrong but that it forecloses…"

**Verify against:** 2007 edition, Chapters 1, 3, and 9; the 1987 preface and the 2007 "New Preface" show the pattern clearly in retrospective framing.

**Diagnostic: when to use interlocutory opening vs. Vidal declarative.** Interlocutory openings suit sections that introduce new theoretical apparatus or empirical evidence and position it against an existing claim. Sections that draw verdicts or develop implications from material already established work better in the Vidal-cartographer mode: a declarative opening that states the claim, followed by evidence or elaboration. Attempting to retrofit interlocutory openings onto verdict/implication sections (§4 and §5 equivalents) typically produces a false "What the analysis reveals is…" construction that is neither genuine Suchman nor genuine Vidal. Prefer Vidal declarative for closing and implication sections; reserve interlocutory pivot for sections where a theoretical position is being named and contested.

---

### Move 2 — Hedged-but-committed first person

**Pattern.** Use *I* at the argumentative hinge — the sentence where a new position is being taken — with a hedging verb (*want to suggest*, *argue*, *propose*) that marks the commitment as an assertion rather than a report.

**Why it matters.** The hedge is not weakness; it is a precise scoping device. "I want to suggest X" signals: this is my position, not a consensus, and I am claiming X specifically and not more than X. This is distinct from the Pattern C anti-pattern ("the essay argues") because the first-person hinge owns the claim rather than attributing it to the text as an object.

**Signature constructions:**
- "I want to argue that…"
- "My aim here is to…" (followed immediately by a specific, finite claim)
- "What I am proposing, then, is…"
- "I take this to mean…" (used when making an interpretive move explicit)

**Verify against:** 2007 edition, Chapter 2 ("Plans") opening; Chapter 7 section on "Situated Action and Plans Reconsidered."

---

### Move 3 — Cumulative-to-claim sentence architecture

**Pattern.** Load qualifying material — circumstantial clauses, participial phrases, hedged conditionals — into the body of the sentence before the main clause, so the qualifications narrow and earn the claim rather than retreating from it. The sentence ends on the precise point.

**Why it matters.** This is the Bacon cumulative sentence used at high precision: the elaborative tail has already been laid in the early part of the sentence, so the main clause arrives earned and exact. In Suchman, the qualifications typically enumerate the conditions under which the claim holds, so the sentence performs its own boundary condition.

**Constructed exemplar (Suchman register, not a quote):**
> "If we take seriously the claim that action is inherently situated — that it unfolds in relation to the specific material and social circumstances it encounters, and cannot be fully specified in advance — then what we call a plan is better understood as a resource for action than as its cause."

**Verify against:** 2007 edition, Chapter 3 ("Plans and Situated Actions"), especially the passages distinguishing plans-as-representations from plans-as-resources.

---

### Move 4 — Passive as distributed agency

**Pattern.** Use passive voice deliberately when the phenomenon being described is co-produced by multiple actors, artifacts, or circumstances — not to avoid naming an agent but to represent genuinely distributed production accurately.

**Why it matters.** This is the most easily misread feature of Suchman's style. The package's default heuristic (from Bacon) is to prefer active constructions with human subjects. In Suchman, certain passive constructions are principled choices: when she writes "plans are produced in the course of action," she is not hiding the actor — she is asserting that production is not attributable to a single actor, and that the passive form is the grammatically accurate representation of that.

**Diagnostic question for the Generator and Evaluator:** Is this passive construction deflecting responsibility from a known agent (Bacon's weak passive) or representing distributed co-production (Suchman's principled passive)? If the latter, leave it.

**Signature constructions:**
- "[X] is produced in the course of…" (production is distributed)
- "[X] is constituted through…" (constitution is relational)
- "[X] is made available by…" (availability is situational)

**Verify against:** 2007 edition, Chapter 5 ("The Objectivity of Documents"); any passage on "interactive trouble."

---

### Move 5 — "Rather than" reframing without dismissal

**Pattern.** Use "rather than" constructions to redirect without negating the prior view entirely. The construction typically takes the form: "not [prior formulation] but [reframing]" or "X, rather than [the received characterization], is better understood as [new formulation]."

**Why it matters.** This move is how Suchman avoids the false binary of "prior work is wrong / my work is right." She reframes the prior work's categories rather than rejecting them. The result is an argument that reads as revisionary rather than polemical.

**Signature constructions:**
- "X, rather than being [A], is better understood as [B]."
- "The question is not whether X but how…"
- "…not as [prior framing] but as [revised framing]."

**Distinguish from the §4 anti-pattern "not X but Y."** The package's DETERMINISTIC_CHECKS §4 flags "not X but Y" constructions at density > 2 per paper. Suchman's "rather than" move is related but distinct: the "not X but Y" tic produces stacked antitheses across the paper; the Suchman "rather than" reframing is a single, carefully placed conceptual pivot. The diagnostic is placement and density: one or two such moves per section, where a genuine reframing is occurring, are Suchman register. Multiple per paragraph, with parallel structure, are the LLM tic.

**Verify against:** 2007 edition, Introduction and Chapter 9; 2002 "Located Accountabilities."

---

### Move 6 — "We" for shared epistemic work, "I" for authorial position

**Pattern.** Use *we* when the epistemic move is one the reader and author make together (following an argument, recognizing a pattern, accepting a shared premise). Use *I* when the move is an authorial position that the reader may not yet share.

**Why it matters.** This distinction produces a voice that is collaborative and exact simultaneously. "We can see that…" invites the reader to confirm a joint observation; "I want to argue that…" marks the point where Suchman is asking the reader to follow her to a new position. Writers who use *we* for everything sound presumptuous; those who use *I* for everything sound isolated. Suchman's alternation gives the reader a map of when they are being guided versus when they are being asked to commit.

**Verify against:** 2007 Chapters 2–3 for dense examples of the alternation.

---

### Move 7 — Paragraph close as open question or reframing, not synthesis

**Pattern.** End paragraphs not with a synthesis statement that wraps up the argument but with a question or reframing that points forward — creating forward drive without prematurely closing the analysis.

**Why it matters.** This is the opposite of the Pattern B anti-pattern ("This X illustrates/supports the argument"). Where the LLM tic announces that the evidence has confirmed something, Suchman's close opens a new angle. The paragraph feels generative rather than completed. Combined with the interlocutory pivot at the opening of the next paragraph, this produces prose that reads as thought in motion rather than position delivered.

**Constructed exemplar (Suchman register, not a quote):**
> "The question this leaves open is how the stable appearance of a plan — its character as something that could have been followed — is itself produced in retrospect, as part of the account we give of action after it has occurred."

**Verify against:** 2007 edition, Chapter 3, paragraph-level structure throughout.

## 3. What the Suchman register is NOT

The Suchman register should not be confused with:

- **Defensive hedging.** Hedges in Suchman narrow claims; they do not retreat from them. If a hedge makes the sentence say less than the author means, it is not Suchman — it is timidity.
- **Impersonal academic prose.** Suchman uses first person at hinges. A passage that replaces every "I" with "this paper" or "one" is in the wrong register (see also Pattern C anti-pattern, `DETERMINISTIC_CHECKS.md §4`).
- **Rhetorical buildup without precise claim.** The cumulative sentence architecture (Move 3) earns a precise claim at the end. If the qualifications accumulate and the main clause is vague, the sentence has the shape of the Suchman move but not its content.

## 4. Register summary for the Generator

When a project declares a Suchman-interlocutor register, the Generator should:

1. **Open sections by naming the position being refined**, not by announcing what the section will do.
2. **Use "I want to argue/suggest" at the argumentative hinge**, not "this paper argues" and not "I" scattered throughout.
3. **Allow passive constructions where production is genuinely distributed**; audit each passive for whether it is deflection (fix it) or representation of distributed agency (keep it).
4. **Use "rather than" for conceptual pivots** — once or twice per section, where a real reframing is occurring.
5. **End paragraphs with a forward-opening question or reframing** rather than a synthesis close or an announcement of what the evidence confirms.
6. **Vary we/I** according to whether the move is shared epistemic work or an authorial position.

---

## 5. Register summary for the Evaluator

When verifying whether a passage achieves the Suchman register, check:

| Feature | What to look for | Fail condition |
|---|---|---|
| Interlocutory positioning | Section opens by naming a prior view | Section opens with roadmap announcement or pure context |
| Hedged-but-committed *I* | "I want to argue/suggest" at the main claim | "The essay argues" (Pattern C) or no first-person at all |
| Cumulative-to-claim architecture | Qualifying material precedes a precise main clause | Qualifications accumulate with no precise payoff |
| Principled passive | Passive used for distributed agency | Passive used to avoid naming the actor |
| "Rather than" pivot | One or two per section at genuine reframings | Stacked "not X but Y" across the paragraph (flag via DETERMINISTIC_CHECKS §4) |
| Forward-opening paragraph close | Paragraph ends on a question or reframing | Paragraph ends with a meta-commentary announcement ("This illustrates…") |

## 6. Constructed exemplars in the Suchman register

The following sentences demonstrate the named features. They are constructed to illustrate the pattern and are **not direct quotes**. Use them as targets for the Generator, not as source material.

**Interlocutory pivot + "rather than" reframing:**
> "The difficulty with treating plans as programs for action is not that plans are unreal but that their relationship to the actions they are said to govern is far less determinate than the programming metaphor suggests; action, rather than executing a plan, is produced in relation to one."

**Cumulative-to-claim with hedged *I*:**
> "If the stability of a design depends on the ongoing work of those who use it — work that remains invisible as long as the design functions smoothly — then I want to argue that accountability for that stability belongs not only to the designers but to the situated practices of use that continually reproduce it."

**Principled passive + "we" for shared observation:**
> "In each of these cases, the relevant categories are not given in advance of the situation but are produced in the course of it; when we look closely, what we find is that the appearance of prior determination is itself an accomplishment."

**Forward-opening paragraph close:**
> "The question this raises — and which the next section takes up — is how the asymmetry between the machine's categories and the user's situation gets managed, and at whose expense."
