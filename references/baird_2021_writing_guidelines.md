# Baird (2021): Guidelines Extracted from *On Writing Research Articles Well*

**Source:** Baird, A. (2021). On writing research articles well: A guide for writing IS papers. *Journal of the Association for Information Systems*, *22*(5), 1197–1211. https://doi.org/10.17705/1jais.00711  

**Note:** This file summarizes the editorial’s actionable guidance. **Worksheets and detailed outlines** are in the original PDF appendices (Planning worksheets A & C; initial outlines B & D). Text was extracted from the PDF for accuracy.

---

## 1. Overall aims for the paper

- The article should be **enjoyable to read** and should **change what readers know** by the time they finish, **without being cognitively taxing** (Hollenbeck, 2008, cited in editorial).
- Use two organizing images:
  - **Red thread** (Savage & Yeh, 2019): a **focused, continuously connected** line of reasoning from start to finish; arguments should not fray.
  - **Hourglass** (Cals & Kotz, 2013; Schulte, 2003): start **broad** (what the audience already knows), **narrow** (what you did and why), then **broaden again** (application, generalizations, opportunities).

Grammar and sentence-level craft: use other sources (e.g. Strunk & White; Zinsser); this editorial focuses on **how** to structure **IS** papers.

## 2. Five things an IS *theory* paper should communicate

Baird recommends drafting **a couple of sentences per area** first; use **Appendix A** in the original paper for a planning worksheet. The five areas do **not** always map one-to-one to manuscript sections (e.g. tension appears in Introduction/Background; resolution may split across “Theory Building Approach” and “New Theory Development”).

| # | Area | Communicate… |
|---|------|----------------|
| **1** | **Area of theoretical focus** | Target **audience (IS subcommunity)**; why they will find the topic **helpful and interesting**. |
| **2** | **Relevant background** | **Common ground** for that audience. Move from **current consensus / what is known** (Barney, 2018; Rai, 2018). |
| **3** | **Theoretical tension** | Why **new theory** is needed. Two routes: (1) *gap spotting* (what is missing?) and (2) ***assumption challenging*** (which assumptions to revisit?) — **prefer assumption challenging**, incl. *heterogeneity where homogeneity is assumed* (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011; Chatterjee & Davison, 2020). |
| **4** | **Resolution of theoretical tension** | How theorizing **resolves** the tension; **objective** of theorizing; **theory-building (extending) approach**; **conditions** under which theory is developed (boundary). |
| **5** | **Guidelines for application** | **Steps** for future researchers applying the work; **future research questions**; how the work could be **extended**. |

**Drafting order (theory papers):** **Write from the middle out** for the first draft: **Background → Theory Building Approach → New Theory Development** first; then **Guidelines**, **Conclusion**, **Abstract**, **Introduction** last. (A quick intro draft upfront is OK; **polish Introduction** late.) **Appendix B** gives an initial outline template.

## 3. Lessons from the Baird & Maruping (2021) theorizing process

- **Iterative:** individual cycles of read → ideate → reread; joint cycles on outlines, tables, figures, drafts, feedback.
- **Reading + synthesis:** anchor new ideas in what is known; **innovation** often comes from **(1) challenging assumptions** and **(2) combining known ideas in new ways**, not only brand-new concepts from scratch (Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2014, as cited).
- **Look beyond** core IS journals when useful (e.g. general-interest science/tech writing) for **examples** of how contemporary artifacts differ.

**Reviewer-facing questions to answer clearly:**

| Lens | Questions |
|------|-----------|
| **Who / What** | **Who** are you speaking to? **What** is your message to that **subcommunity**? (Avoid trying to address the **entire IS field** at once.) Pick subcommunity by: relevant **theory base** to build on/challenge + who is **most impacted/receptive**. |
| **Why** | Why this focus, this perspective, **framework vs explanatory model**, this construct/term vs alternatives, **why sufficiently new**? Surface **trade-offs** at major decision points (not every micro-choice). |
| **When / Where** | **Boundary conditions:** when does the theory apply / not apply? Tie to **assumptions challenged**; explain **primary** boundaries so reviewers can evaluate (Busse et al., 2017; Corley & Gioia, 2011; Whetten, 1989). |
| **How** | **How** should the framework be **applied** (e.g. guidelines + examples for doctoral students/future papers)? Link **future research** to **discarded options** from major choices—not a disconnected laundry list. |

## 4. Generalized process for IS papers (including empirical): nine steps

### Step 1: Core message (five areas — parallel to theory paper)

Use **Appendix C** worksheet for empirical papers.

1. **Area of focus:** Context **interesting** to target audience; what part of their **mental model** to connect to.  
2. **Background:** Literature for **that** subset; **common ground**; use **string citations** (“we know X [refs], Y [refs], Z [refs]”).  
3. **Tension:** **Most important** part — reader should feel the tension is **worth solving** and must read on.  
4. **Resolution:** Objective, theory base, **highlights** of design/methods — **match community norms**.  
5. **Contribution:** **Second most important** — does it warrant reading the whole paper? How does it move **consensus** forward?

These five areas often become a **five-paragraph Introduction** outline; exact mapping to later sections may differ (e.g. Background + Tension combined; Resolution → Methods + Results).

### Step 2: Outline using target journal format

Use **Appendix D** as a guide; **mirror first-choice journal** structure, headings, references, limits (e.g. abstract word count).

### Step 3: Introduction bullets

One bullet per area; add 2–3 sub-bullets each → later one paragraph per area.

### Step 4: Literature synthesis table(s)

- Columns: **Research area | Description | Citations** (Table 2 pattern).  
- One **synthesizing paragraph per row** afterward.  
- **Perspective:** lit review is an **evidence-based editorial**, not a fact list.  
- Cite **classics and recent** work.  
- **Rookie mistakes:** listing everything read; summarizing studies **without** tying them to **objective and focal tensions**.

### Step 5: Results tables/figures + methods detail

- Build **primary results** (often ≤ ~5 tables/figures) **before** writing full paper; **emulate structure** of a model paper (same design), not copy text.  
- **Tip:** Finalize primary results **before** full draft to avoid repeated rewrites.  
- **Theory, design, data, methods** must **match** and stay **consistent**.  
- **Results:** stay **factual**; interpretation mainly in **Discussion** unless needed for clarity.  
- **Rookie mistake:** “Surprise” constructs/relationships first appearing in **Results** — introduce in Background or Methods.

### Step 6: Discussion outline

Bullets for **primary theory/practice contributions** and **limitations** (and future research if desired). Use realization gaps to **revisit analyses** or **refine contribution message**.

**Terminology:** *“Synonyms are the enemy of the theorist”* (Rivard, in workshop cited). Avoid **new near-synonym labels** late (especially Discussion); use terms introduced earlier or add concepts **earlier** in the paper.

### Step 7: Draft body (middle out)

Prefer **agile** co-author iteration (draft sections, share, revise) over **waterfall** (single finished dump).

### Step 8: Abstract, Introduction, Discussion (drafts)

After core message is stable. **Abstract + Introduction = executive summaries**: **not** a slow mystery; **not** hiding contribution until the end. **Shorter sentences**; avoid piling conjunctions. Align **full outline** with these sections.

### Step 9: Full pass — reread and **tighten**

Remove **extraneous** text; strengthen **red thread**; fix confusion. If **you** are bored or lost, revise. Use **Zinsser**-style guidance; read **at least three times** before finalizing. Revise incrementally; perfection obsession not required in early drafts.

**Deviation:** Section structures may vary (Background, Results, Discussion) — **do not confuse readers**; minimize **cognitive effort**.

## 5. Quick reference: tips vs rookie mistakes (as listed)

| Tips | Rookie mistakes |
|------|-----------------|
| String citations for efficient background | Lit review = unrelated laundry list |
| Tie every summary to your purpose/tension | Study summaries with no link to your objective |
| Results known before full narrative draft | Surprise constructs in Results |
| Future research from guidelines & discarded options | Disconnected future-research laundry list |
| Shorter sentences; intro states point early | Long chained sentences; cliffhanger intro |
